A _structure–activity relationship_ (SAR) is the observed pattern linking changes in a molecule's chemical structure to changes in what it does — its potency, its selectivity, its behaviour in the body. It is the central working knowledge of medicinal chemistry: the accumulated answer to "if I change this part of the molecule, what happens?"

SAR is built empirically, one change at a time. Chemists make a series of closely related variants — "analogues" — of a compound, adding a chemical group here, swapping an atom there, then measure each in the same [[Assay|assays]] and tabulate how each structural change shifted activity. Patterns emerge: one chemical group turns out to be essential for binding; enlarging a certain region improves potency up to a point; a particular position can be altered freely without losing activity. This map is what lets optimization be _steered_ rather than guessed — chemists design the next molecule from what the trend predicts.

A relationship can be _steep_ ("any change here destroys activity") or _flat_ ("changes here barely matter"), and both are useful: the flat regions are exactly where a molecule can be modified to fix its other properties — solubility, stability, absorption — without sacrificing potency. A SAR is specific to one target and one chemical series; a lesson learned for one scaffold rarely carries over wholesale to another, so a fresh series means rebuilding the relationship from scratch.
