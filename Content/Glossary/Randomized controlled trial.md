A _randomized controlled trial_ (RCT) is the most rigorous way to find out whether a treatment actually works, by comparing people who get it against a similar group who do not, with the two groups made comparable through chance. It is the design that decisive clinical trials rest on, because it removes the biases that make other kinds of evidence untrustworthy.

It has three defining features. _Control_: the treatment group is compared against a control group receiving either a placebo (a dummy with no active drug) or the current standard care, so the treatment's effect can be separated from natural recovery or the mere act of being treated. _Randomization_: each participant is assigned to a group purely by chance, which — across enough people — balances the groups for every other factor, known and unknown (age, severity, lifestyle), so the only systematic difference between them is the treatment. _Blinding_: wherever possible, neither the participants nor the staff assessing them know who got what, which stops expectations from colouring how people report symptoms or how outcomes are judged.

On top of these, the trial is _powered_ — made large enough that a real effect of the expected size will show up as more than chance — and its success measure (the [[Clinical endpoint|endpoint]]) is fixed in advance. Together these make an RCT's result hard to argue with: a difference between the groups can be attributed to the treatment itself, not to bias or coincidence. The price is cost, time, and the ethics of withholding a possibly-helpful treatment from the control group. The approach was pioneered in the Medical Research Council's 1948 streptomycin trial for tuberculosis, the first modern RCT.
