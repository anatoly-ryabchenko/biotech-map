A _promoter_ is a stretch of DNA sitting just in front of a gene that controls whether, where, and how strongly that gene is switched on. A gene is only useful if it is _read_ — transcribed into RNA and then made into protein — and the promoter is the switch and dial for that reading: the cell's gene-reading machinery docks at the promoter to begin, so the promoter's sequence governs how often reading starts, and therefore how much protein is produced.

Crucially, promoters differ in their behaviour. Some are _strong_, driving high output; others are weak. Some are _ubiquitous_, active in essentially every cell type; others are _tissue-specific_, switching the gene on only in, say, liver cells or muscle cells, because they respond to regulatory signals present only there. This is what makes the promoter a central design choice when building a genetic medicine: by choosing the promoter placed in front of a therapeutic gene, developers _aim_ the therapy — making it produce its protein strongly everywhere, or only in the one tissue where it is wanted and nowhere else.

In gene therapy the promoter is part of the genetic payload (the _expression cassette_) packed into the delivery vehicle, and choosing it trades against the vehicle's tight size limit: a powerful, tissue-specific promoter is desirable but consumes precious space that could otherwise hold more of the gene itself.
