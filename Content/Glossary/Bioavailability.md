_Bioavailability_ is the fraction of an administered dose that actually reaches the bloodstream in active form — and so is available to act on the body. By definition a drug injected straight into a vein is fully (100%) bioavailable; the term matters most for drugs that must first be _absorbed_, above all pills taken by mouth, where much of a dose can be lost on the way.

For an oral drug the journey carries two big losses. First, the drug must _dissolve_ in the gut and then _cross the gut wall_ into the blood — a compound that barely dissolves, or cannot permeate the wall, is poorly absorbed, which is why so much formulation effort goes into solubility. Second, blood leaving the gut passes through the liver before reaching the rest of the body, and the liver may chemically destroy a large share on this _first pass_ — so even a fully absorbed drug can arrive depleted. Bioavailability is written as a percentage (often "F"): an F of 30% means only a third of the swallowed dose reaches circulation intact.

Low or erratic bioavailability is a common reason a promising compound fails as an oral drug — if too little gets in, or the fraction swings unpredictably between patients or with food, the dose cannot be controlled. The physical properties that govern it (size, solubility, fattiness, vulnerability to liver enzymes) are captured by rules of thumb such as Lipinski's "rule of five," and are a major focus of optimization and formulation. Bioavailability is one outcome of a drug's overall [[ADME|ADME]] behaviour.
