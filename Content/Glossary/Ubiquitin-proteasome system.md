Every cell must constantly destroy proteins — ones that are damaged, no longer needed, or meant to be short-lived — and it does this mainly through the _ubiquitin–proteasome system_, its route for controlled protein destruction. The system has two halves: a tagging step and a shredding step.

The tag is _ubiquitin_, a small protein. A chain of enzymes works in sequence to attach ubiquitin molecules onto a doomed protein, building up a short ubiquitin chain that acts as a "destroy me" label. The decisive, selective step is performed by an [[E3 ligase|E3 ubiquitin ligase]] — the enzyme that recognizes _which_ protein gets tagged. There are hundreds of different E3 ligases, each picking out particular targets, and this is what gives the system its specificity.

The shredder is the _proteasome_, a large barrel-shaped machine. It recognizes the ubiquitin tag, grabs the labelled protein, unfolds it, and feeds it through its hollow core, where the protein is chopped into short fragments and the ubiquitin tags are released to be reused. Together this lets a cell keep its proteins under tight, fast control.

This machinery is exactly what protein-degrader drugs hijack. A degrader does not inhibit its target; it recruits one of the cell's E3 ligases to tag the target with ubiquitin, so the proteasome destroys it — borrowing the cell's own disposal system to remove a disease protein. Because a degrader only triggers the tagging and is then released, it works catalytically; and because it leans on the cell's machinery, its effect depends on how much E3 ligase and proteasome a given cell happens to have.
