A _chimeric antigen receptor_ (CAR) is a synthetic receptor — built from parts of several different proteins stitched together, hence "chimeric" — that is engineered into a patient's immune cells, usually T cells, to make them recognize and kill a chosen target, most often a marker on a cancer cell. It is the heart of CAR-T cell therapy.

Its design mirrors what a T cell has to do: see a target outside, and fire inside. The outward-facing end is a _binder_ — typically an _scFv_, the two gripping tips of a [[Monoclonal antibody|monoclonal antibody]] fused into one compact unit — chosen to latch onto a surface marker on the diseased cell. This connects, through a _hinge_ and a segment that spans the cell membrane, to signalling parts inside the cell. The core "fire" signal comes from a piece called _CD3ζ_, borrowed from the T cell's own natural activation machinery. Added to it are one or two _costimulatory_ domains (from proteins called 4-1BB or CD28) — the crucial second signal that sets how strongly the cell switches on, how vigorously it multiplies, and how long it survives afterward: 4-1BB tends to give slower, longer-lived cells, CD28 faster and more intense ones.

Because the binder reads a surface marker directly — rather than the processed fragments a natural T-cell receptor sees — a CAR can be aimed at any accessible surface protein and works regardless of the patient's tissue type. Its great hazard is the binder's target: since the cell kills everything bearing that marker, a target also present on healthy tissue causes serious harm — so the choice of what the CAR points at is the central safety question.
