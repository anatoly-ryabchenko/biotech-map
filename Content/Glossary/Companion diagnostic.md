A _companion diagnostic_ is a test that must be run before a particular drug is given, to identify the patients in whom it is likely to work (or in whom it would be unsafe). It is paired with the drug — the drug's label may require the test — so the medicine is given only to people whose biology matches it.

The need arises because many modern drugs are _targeted_: they act on one specific molecular feature and only help patients whose disease actually has that feature. A cancer drug aimed at a particular mutated protein, for example, works only in tumours carrying that mutation; giving it to patients whose tumours lack it exposes them to side effects and cost for no benefit, and delays them from a treatment that might help. A companion diagnostic checks the patient's tissue, blood, or tumour for the relevant marker — a mutation, a protein on the cell surface, a gene's activity — and sorts patients into those who should and should not receive the drug.

This is the practical machinery of "precision medicine": matching the right drug to the right patient by measuring the target first. In some modalities the matching is built in — a radiopharmaceutical's imaging scan both finds the disease and confirms it takes up the drug before the therapeutic dose is given, a [[Theranostics|theranostic]] pairing. Because the test directly gates who gets treated, a companion diagnostic is developed and validated alongside the drug, and the two are often reviewed and approved together.
