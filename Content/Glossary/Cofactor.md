A cofactor is a non-protein helper that an enzyme needs in order to do its job. An enzyme is a protein that carries out a chemical reaction — often one that would barely proceed at all without it — but the twenty amino acids a protein is built from can only supply a limited range of chemistry. Many reactions need something the amino acids cannot provide: a metal atom, or a specialised small molecule. That extra piece is the cofactor. An enzyme missing its cofactor is inert and is called an *apoenzyme*; once the cofactor is in place, the complete, working form is called a *holoenzyme*.

Cofactors come in two broad kinds. The first is **metal ions** — charged metal atoms such as zinc, iron, magnesium, copper, or manganese, held in the active site (the pocket where the reaction happens). Metals are chemically versatile in ways amino acids are not: they can pull electrons toward themselves to weaken a bond that needs breaking, hold a passing [[Substrate|substrate]] in exactly the right orientation, steady the strained, half-rearranged *transition state* the reaction must pass through, or shuttle electrons in and out during reactions that move charge. The second kind is **coenzymes** — small organic molecules, frequently built by the body from vitamins. A coenzyme typically acts as a carrier: it picks up a chemical group or a pair of electrons from one reaction and delivers them to another. NAD is a common example — it collects electrons from one molecule, drifts off carrying them, hands them to a different enzyme elsewhere, then returns to be used again. Some cofactors are bound permanently to their enzyme (a *prosthetic group*); others dock, do one round of work, and leave.

A great many enzymes — including most that handle energy, build or break down molecules, or carry out the harder chemical transformations — simply do not function without the right cofactor, so the cofactor is part of the enzyme's identity, not an optional extra. This shapes how new enzymes are built. Designing an enzyme that uses a cofactor means designing the active site to cradle *both* the substrate and the cofactor at once, holding them in the precise geometry where the cofactor's chemistry can act on the substrate — harder than positioning a substrate alone, and exactly why some protein-design tools must shape a backbone around an explicit small-molecule cofactor rather than around protein contacts only. The same logic appears in medicine: some drugs work by mimicking or blocking a cofactor, and producing cofactor-dependent enzymes at scale means supplying the cofactor too.
