_In vitro transcription_ (IVT) is the way mRNA drugs are made: a cell-free chemical reaction in which an enzyme reads a DNA template and copies it into many RNA strands — "in vitro" meaning it runs in a tube, not inside a living cell. It is the manufacturing engine of the entire mRNA modality.

The reaction is simple in outline. A DNA template carrying the desired sequence is mixed in a tube with an _RNA polymerase_ (an enzyme that builds RNA, typically borrowed from a bacteriophage), the four RNA building blocks (or their [[Modified nucleosides|modified]] versions), and supporting chemicals. The polymerase latches onto the start of the template and travels along it, stringing together the matching RNA letters and releasing a finished mRNA strand — and it does this over and over, so one template yields many copies. The strand's protective cap and tail are added either during the reaction or just after.

IVT is what makes mRNA uniquely fast and flexible to produce: switching to a new drug needs only a new DNA template, with the same reaction and equipment — which is why mRNA vaccines could be designed and produced at extraordinary speed. The main challenges come after the reaction, in purifying the product — especially removing a troublesome byproduct, _double-stranded RNA_, that the polymerase makes by accident and that strongly triggers the cell's immune alarms.
