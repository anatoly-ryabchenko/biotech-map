An _assay_ is a controlled laboratory test that measures whether — and how strongly — something happens, and turns it into a number. In drug work an assay is how a question like "does this compound act on the target?" becomes data: the target (a protein, a living cell, sometimes a whole organism) is combined with the test compound under fixed conditions, and a _readout_ — a signal that can be measured — reports the result. The readout might be light from a dye that glows when the target is active, a colour change, a radioactive count, or cells living or dying.

A good assay is built around a clear, specific signal that genuinely tracks the thing being studied, with _controls_ on every run: a sample with no compound, to show the baseline, and one with a known active, to show the full effect, so each result is judged against both. Compounds are usually tested across a range of concentrations to build a _dose–response curve_, from which a single summarizing number is read — the concentration that produces half the maximum effect, written _IC50_ for an inhibitor or _EC50_ for an activator. Lower means more potent.

The central hazard is the _false signal_: many things can move a readout without the compound truly acting on the target — the compound might itself glow or quench light, clump and sequester the target, react non-specifically, or simply kill the cells. So a result from one assay is confirmed in a second, independent assay with a different readout before it is believed. Assays run at every stage of drug development, from the first hunt for a hit to the batch-release tests that decide whether manufactured drug can be shipped.
