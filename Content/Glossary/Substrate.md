A substrate is the molecule an enzyme acts on. An enzyme is a protein that carries out one particular chemical reaction: it grabs a starting molecule — the substrate — chemically rearranges it, and lets go of the result as a new molecule called the *product*. So "substrate" and "product" name the two ends of an enzyme's work: substrate goes in, product comes out. (More broadly the word means whatever molecule a catalyst acts on, but in biology it almost always means the input of an enzyme.)

An enzyme does far more than hurry a reaction that would happen anyway. Many of the reactions enzymes run would, left alone, proceed so slowly as to effectively never occur on a timescale that matters to a living cell. The enzyme is what makes them go at all — and it does so not by adding heat or force but by binding the substrate, holding it in a precise orientation, bringing chemically reactive groups to bear on it, and often flexing through a set sequence of its own shape changes as the reaction proceeds. Take the enzyme away and, in many cases, the reaction simply does not run.

The substrate binds at a specific pocket on the enzyme called the *active site* — a small region whose shape and chemistry are complementary to the substrate, so the two come together and hold; this is a binding event of the kind described in [[docking]]. That fit is the source of an enzyme's *specificity*: because only a molecule matching the active site binds well, each enzyme works on its own substrate (or a small family of close relatives) and leaves the thousands of other molecules around it alone. The match is usually not perfectly rigid — enzyme and substrate adjust their shapes slightly as they meet, settling into a tighter fit.

Binding is only the setup; the active site then drives the chemistry. Turning substrate into product means passing through a strained, half-rearranged arrangement of atoms partway between the two — the *transition state* — and reaching that arrangement is the slow, costly barrier of any reaction. The active site grips the substrate and arranges its reactive groups so that this transition state is reached far more easily, which is how an enzyme accelerates its reaction by many orders of magnitude. Once the substrate has been converted, the product no longer fits the active site as snugly, so it leaves, and the enzyme — itself unchanged by the reaction — is free to take on the next substrate molecule and repeat.

The substrate is the reference point for almost everything said about an enzyme. An enzyme's identity is "what substrate it converts, and into what"; its quality is judged by how fast and how selectively it handles that substrate. When a brand-new enzyme is built ([[in silico]] protein design), the central difficulty is shaping an active site that not only binds the intended substrate but holds it and the catalytic groups in the exact geometry that drives the reaction — which is why designed enzymes are checked with detailed physical simulations of the active site working on its substrate. Drugs use the same idea in reverse: many medicines are *inhibitors* that mimic an enzyme's substrate closely enough to occupy its active site without being converted, jamming the enzyme and shutting its reaction down.
