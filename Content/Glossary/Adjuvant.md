An _adjuvant_ is an ingredient added to a vaccine whose job is not to be recognized itself, but to _amplify and shape_ the immune system's response to the vaccine's [[Antigen|antigen]]. A purified antigen given alone is often too quiet to provoke strong, lasting protection — the immune system needs, alongside it, a "danger" signal saying this is worth reacting to. The adjuvant supplies that signal.

Adjuvants work in two broad ways, often combined. Some are _immunostimulants_: molecules that trip the immune system's built-in alarms — the pattern-recognition sensors (such as Toll-like receptors) that evolved to detect pathogens — switching on the antigen-presenting cells that kick off a response. Others are _delivery systems_: carriers that hold the antigen at the injection site, release it slowly, and shuttle it to immune cells, prolonging and concentrating the exposure. Real adjuvants range from the old standby _alum_ (aluminium salts, which mainly favour an antibody response), through oil-in-water emulsions like MF59, to potent modern combinations such as AS01 (a Toll-like-receptor-triggering bacterial molecule plus a plant saponin) that drive the strong, T-cell-rich responses some diseases demand.

The defining tension is _power versus tolerability_: a stronger adjuvant gives better and longer protection but also more soreness, fever, and a higher risk of overshooting into harmful inflammation. Because vaccines are given to vast numbers of healthy people, often children, the safety bar is far higher than for a drug given to the sick — so the art is choosing the weakest adjuvant that still protects. (mRNA and live-vector vaccines often need no separate adjuvant, because the delivery itself already alarms the immune system.)
