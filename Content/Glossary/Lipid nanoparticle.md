A _lipid nanoparticle_ (LNP) is a tiny fatty sphere — far smaller than a cell — used to wrap a fragile genetic cargo and smuggle it into cells. It is the delivery vehicle that finally made mRNA drugs and some in-body gene-editing therapies work, because a bare genetic strand is destroyed in the blood within minutes and cannot cross into cells on its own.

An LNP is built from a few kinds of fat in set proportions. The key one is an _ionizable lipid_: it is electrically neutral while circulating in the blood (which keeps the particle non-toxic and stealthy), but turns positively charged in acidic surroundings. The other ingredients — a helper lipid, cholesterol for rigidity, and a small amount of a polymer-coated (PEG) lipid that sets the particle's size and circulation time — complete the shell, with the genetic cargo trapped inside. The particle is made by forcing the lipids and the cargo together through a fast micro-mixer, so the lipids snap shut around the cargo.

Two features define how an LNP behaves. First, _endosomal escape_: after a cell swallows the particle into an internal bubble that would normally digest it, the bubble acidifies, the ionizable lipid charges up and breaks the bubble's membrane, and the cargo spills into the cell's interior where it can work — a step that is strikingly inefficient (often only a small percentage of cargo escapes) and a major focus of research. Second, _where they go_: LNPs given into the blood naturally pile up in the liver, so reaching any other organ is hard and limits which diseases these modalities can treat. LNPs are also fragile, often needing deep-freeze storage — the reason the first mRNA vaccines demanded ultra-cold logistics. The ionizable-lipid design behind today's LNPs was established by Semple and colleagues in 2010.
