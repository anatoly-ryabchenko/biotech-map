_Phage display_ is a method for fishing rare binding molecules out of enormous libraries by physically linking each candidate molecule to the gene that encodes it. The link is a _bacteriophage_ — a virus that infects bacteria: the gene for a candidate protein or peptide is fused into one of the phage's own coat-protein genes, so the phage _displays_ that candidate on its surface while carrying the encoding gene inside. Display and code travel together in one particle.

To use it, a library of billions of phage — each showing a different peptide or antibody fragment — is poured over the immobilized target. Phage whose displayed molecule binds stick; the vast non-binding majority are washed away. The bound phage are recovered, multiplied by letting them infect bacteria, and the selection is repeated. Each round of this _panning_ enriches the binders until a small set of strong, specific ones dominates — and because each carries its own gene, the winners are identified simply by sequencing.

The power of phage display is that it searches libraries far larger than could ever be tested one by one, entirely in a test tube, with no animal required, and lets the target be chosen precisely. It is a workhorse for discovering peptides and for finding and refining antibody fragments. Its main limits: only molecules the phage can fold and display work well, and binding while stuck on a phage surface does not always predict how the free molecule behaves, so hits are re-made and re-tested. George Smith introduced the method in 1985.
