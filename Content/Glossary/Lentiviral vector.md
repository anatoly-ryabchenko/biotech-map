A _lentiviral vector_ is a gene-delivery vehicle built from a stripped-down lentivirus (the family that includes HIV), engineered so it can ferry a chosen gene into a cell but can no longer replicate or cause disease. Its defining feature is that it _integrates_: it permanently splices its cargo gene into the cell's own chromosomes, so the gene becomes a fixed part of the cell's DNA and is inherited by every descendant when the cell divides.

That permanence is exactly what makes lentivirus the standard tool for engineering _cells_ — as in CAR-T and other cell therapies — where the new gene (such as the gene for an artificial receptor) must stay in the cell and all its progeny for the therapy to keep working. Lentiviral vectors also carry more genetic cargo than [[Adeno-associated virus|AAV]], and they can deliver genes into non-dividing cells, which earlier gene-delivery viruses could not.

The price of integration is risk. Because the vector inserts into the genome, where it lands is not fully controlled, and an insertion near a growth-controlling gene can switch that gene on and seed a cancer — the danger of [[Insertional mutagenesis|insertional mutagenesis]]. Modern lentiviral vectors are heavily redesigned to reduce this (and made unable to reassemble into a live virus), and they tend to insert in somewhat safer places than the older retroviral vectors that caused leukaemias in early gene-therapy trials. The use of an HIV-based vector to deliver genes even to non-dividing cells was established by Naldini and colleagues in 1996.
