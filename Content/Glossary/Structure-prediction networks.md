A structure-prediction network is a machine-learning model that takes an amino-acid sequence and forecasts the three-dimensional shape that sequence would really fold into (see [[protein folding principles]] for why a sequence settles into a particular shape). It answers the question "if this protein existed, what would it look like?" without anyone ever making it. AlphaFold2 and its successor AlphaFold3 (DeepMind, 2021 and 2024) and ESMFold (Meta, 2022) are the leading examples. AlphaFold builds its prediction partly from evolutionary information — collections of related natural sequences — while ESMFold predicts from the single sequence alone using a large language model trained on proteins, which makes it faster but somewhat less accurate on hard cases.

These models also report how confident they are, position by position. The common measure is *pLDDT* (predicted local distance difference test), a per-residue score from 0 to 100: above roughly 90 means the local shape is predicted with high confidence, 70–90 means the backbone is reliable, and below 50 marks regions the model thinks are floppy or simply cannot place. This confidence is itself one of the signals used to judge a design (see [[protein properties prediction]]).

In [[in silico]] protein design, structure prediction does two jobs. First, *does it fold?* — the sequence proposed by an [[inverse-folding networks]] model is run through, and the design is kept only if the predicted shape closely matches the shape it was designed for, and discarded or revised otherwise, before any lab work. Second, *does it bind the right things?* — the designed protein is folded *together* with its intended target and with negative targets (molecules it must avoid), to confirm it binds the target and not the rest (see [[docking]] and [[negative design]]).

There is a known failure mode the whole pipeline has to account for. These models are trained largely on natural protein structures, most captured as a single static snapshot, so they carry little sense of the conditions a protein actually lives in and are implicitly biased toward shapes evolution has already explored — and a genuinely novel design sits exactly where that training gives the least guidance. Worse, structure-prediction and inverse-folding networks often share training data and assumptions, so a gap in that data — a missing kind of structure, a shortage of negative examples — can produce the *same* mistake in both at once. The inverse-folding model proposes a flawed sequence, the structure-prediction model fails to catch it in the same way, and the design passes its check as a *false positive*. This is the main reason wet-lab validation remains essential and the design loop stays iterative.

Structure prediction is the gatekeeper of computational protein design: the cheap, fast check that lets thousands of candidate designs be screened on a computer so only the most promising reach the slow, expensive lab — but its shared blind spots are also where confidently-scored designs quietly fail.
