A _genome-wide association study_ (GWAS) is a way to find which genes are involved in a disease by scanning the entire genomes of large numbers of people and looking for genetic differences that are more common in those with the disease than in those without it. Rather than guessing which gene to examine, it surveys the whole genome at once and lets the data point to the culprits.

It works by reading, across many thousands of people, the spots in the genome where humans commonly differ by a single DNA letter (called SNPs). Comparing a group with a disease against a group without it, the study asks which letter-variants show up more often in the affected group. A variant that travels with the disease marks a stretch of the genome — and so a nearby gene, and the protein it makes — as likely involved in causing it. Because this means detecting small differences against a noisy background, GWAS needs very large groups (often tens of thousands of people) and strict statistical thresholds to avoid false leads.

GWAS is prized in drug discovery for a specific reason: it reflects a _natural experiment_ in real humans. The genetic variants people are born with were dealt at conception and have shaped their biology over a lifetime, so a variant linked to a disease is strong evidence that the gene genuinely contributes to it — far more trustworthy than a result from a cell line or an animal. This is why human genetic support has become so valued: drug targets backed by it are roughly _two to three times as likely_ to survive clinical trials and win approval. Its main limit is that a GWAS hit points to a region, not always the exact causal gene, so the lead must still be pinned down and validated.
