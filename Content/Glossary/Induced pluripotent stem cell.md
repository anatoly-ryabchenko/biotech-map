An _induced pluripotent stem cell_ (iPSC) is an ordinary adult cell — taken from skin, blood, or even urine — that has been reprogrammed back into a _pluripotent_ state: a cell that can divide indefinitely and turn into essentially any cell type in the body. It is made by forcing the adult cell to switch on a small set of master genes (the "Yamanaka factors") that reset its identity to that of an early-embryo-like cell.

This is powerful for regenerative medicine because it gives an unlimited, ethically uncomplicated source of cells that can be turned into whatever cell type a patient has lost — insulin-making pancreatic cells, dopamine neurons, heart muscle, retina. Starting from a bank of iPSCs, a _differentiation_ protocol — a timed sequence of chemical signals mimicking embryonic development — coaxes the cells, step by step, into the wanted mature type. The cells can be _autologous_ (reprogrammed from the patient's own cells, so immune-matched but bespoke) or _allogeneic_ (from one well-characterized donor line, banked to treat many patients off the shelf).

The defining danger flows from the very property that makes iPSCs useful: their unlimited capacity to divide and become anything. If any _undifferentiated_ iPSCs remain in the final product, they can keep dividing after transplant and form a tumour (a teratoma). So a regenerative product must be driven fully to the target cell type and then exhaustively checked to confirm no pluripotent stragglers remain — the central safety task of the modality. The method for making iPSCs was discovered by Takahashi and Yamanaka in 2006.
