_Glycosylation_ is the attachment of sugar chains to a protein. Many proteins — especially those a cell secretes or displays on its surface, including most antibodies and therapeutic proteins — are decorated, after they are built, with branched trees of sugar molecules at specific points along the chain. These sugars are not incidental: they can make up a large share of the molecule's weight and strongly shape how it behaves.

The sugar chains do several jobs. They help the protein fold and stay stable; they can shield it from being broken down, lengthening how long it survives in the blood; they influence whether the immune system treats the protein as "self" and tolerates it or attacks it; and for some drugs they are part of the function itself — a particular sugar pattern on an antibody can strengthen or weaken its ability to recruit immune killing, and replacement enzymes must carry a specific sugar tag to be taken up by the cells that need them.

Crucially, the exact sugar pattern is added by the _cell_ that makes the protein, using its own machinery, and it differs between species and cell types. This is why biologic drugs are grown in mammalian cells (often Chinese hamster ovary cells) that attach human-like sugars; why small shifts in the manufacturing process can change the sugars, and so the drug; and why production cell lines are deliberately _glycoengineered_ — altered to attach a chosen sugar pattern. Getting glycosylation right, and identical from batch to batch, is one of the central challenges of making protein drugs.
