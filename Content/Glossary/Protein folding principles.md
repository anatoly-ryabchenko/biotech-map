A protein is a chain of amino acids — small building blocks, twenty common kinds, strung together in a particular order. That order is the protein's *sequence*. The chain does not stay strung out; it folds up on itself into a specific three-dimensional shape, and that shape is what gives the protein its function — what it binds, what it does. The central principle of protein folding is that the shape is largely determined by the sequence: the same chain, placed in the same conditions, folds into the same shape.

What drives the folding is a set of physical forces among the amino acids and the surrounding water. The largest is the *hydrophobic effect*: some amino acids are water-repelling (nonpolar), and the chain folds so as to bury these on the inside, away from water, leaving water-friendly ones on the surface. This collapse pulls the chain into a compact globular form. Within that form, *hydrogen bonds* — weak, directional links between certain atoms along the backbone — lock parts of the chain into regular repeating local shapes, chiefly coiled *alpha helices* and flat *beta sheets*; these are the protein's secondary structure. Two more forces fine-tune the result: *electrostatics*, the attraction of opposite charges and repulsion of like charges between amino acids, and *van der Waals* forces, a weak attraction between atoms packed closely together. The balance of all these decides the final fold.

The thermodynamic view ties this together. Every possible arrangement of the chain has an associated free energy — loosely, how strained or relaxed it is — and the chain settles into the arrangement of *lowest* free energy, the most stable one. For a well-behaved protein this lowest-energy arrangement is its working shape, called the *native fold*. The picture is often drawn as an *energy landscape*: a surface of energies over all possible shapes, with the native fold at the deepest point, toward which the chain slides. So saying a shape is a stable, low-energy state for a sequence means: if the chain were placed in that shape, the forces would hold it there rather than let it unravel into something else.

This is why design is hard. Going from sequence to shape — predicting the fold — means finding that lowest point on a vast landscape; this is what [[structure-prediction networks]] attempt. Going the other way, from a desired shape back to a sequence that will adopt it, is the job of [[inverse-folding networks]] — and a sequence can have a *different* shape that is even lower in energy, which it would fold into instead, so a proposed sequence must still be checked. And judging whether two folded molecules bind — [[docking]] — depends entirely on getting these shapes right.
