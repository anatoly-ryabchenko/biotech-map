_RNA interference_ (RNAi) is a natural system inside our cells that silences genes by destroying their messenger RNA — the working copy of a gene that a cell reads in order to make a protein. It evolved partly as a defense against viruses, which often make double-stranded RNA, and it gives cells a way to switch specific genes off. Drug developers borrow it to shut down disease-causing genes on demand.

The trigger is _double-stranded RNA_. When a short double-stranded RNA appears, the cell's machinery loads one of its two strands — the _guide_ — into a protein complex (called RISC) and discards the other. The guide strand then acts as a search template: by base-pairing (A with U, G with C) it finds any messenger RNA whose sequence matches, and the complex cuts that message, destroying it before it can be made into protein. Crucially the complex is _catalytic_ — after cutting one message it moves on to the next, so a small amount of guide clears many copies of the target.

This is the mechanism behind _siRNA_ (small interfering RNA) drugs: a short double-stranded RNA is designed so its guide strand matches the disease gene's message, and once delivered into cells it commandeers the RNAi machinery to silence that gene. Because the guide finds its target purely by sequence, an siRNA can in principle be made against almost any gene — though it must be chemically stabilized and delivered to the right cells to work as a medicine. RNAi was discovered by Fire and Mello in 1998.
