An _epitope_ is the specific small patch on a target molecule that an antibody — or an immune cell's receptor — actually grips. An [[Antigen|antigen]] is usually a whole protein, but an antibody does not bind the whole thing; it recognizes one little region of its surface, perhaps a dozen atoms' worth, and that region is the epitope. A single protein typically carries several distinct epitopes, and different antibodies bind different ones.

The shape and chemistry of the epitope are what give antibody binding its precision: the antibody's binding site is complementary to that exact patch, so it sticks there and almost nowhere else. Epitopes come in two kinds. A _linear_ epitope is a short continuous stretch of the protein's amino-acid sequence. A _conformational_ epitope is formed by parts of the chain that are far apart in sequence but folded close together in the three-dimensional structure — so it exists only while the protein holds its proper shape, and is lost if the protein unfolds or springs into a different form.

Which epitope is engaged matters enormously. In antibody discovery, two antibodies against the same target can behave completely differently depending on the epitope each one hits — one might block the target's function, another do nothing. In vaccine design, the goal is to make the immune system focus on the epitopes whose antibodies actually _neutralize_ the pathogen, and to present them in the right conformation — which is why a viral protein that changes shape can fail as a vaccine if it no longer displays its protective conformational epitopes.
