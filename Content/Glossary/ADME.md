_ADME_ stands for the four things the body does to a drug: _Absorption_ (getting from the dose site — the gut, a muscle — into the bloodstream), _Distribution_ (spreading from blood into tissues, including how much stays stuck to blood proteins versus reaching the target), _Metabolism_ (chemical breakdown, mostly by liver enzymes, into other molecules), and _Excretion_ (removal from the body, chiefly by the kidneys into urine or the liver into bile). Together, ADME decides whether enough drug reaches the right place, at the right concentration, for the right length of time — a compound can be perfectly potent against its target and still fail because its ADME is wrong.

These processes are quantified by _pharmacokinetics_: measuring the drug's concentration in the blood over time and extracting numbers that summarize its fate. The key ones are the peak concentration reached (_Cmax_), the total exposure over time (the area under the concentration–time curve, _AUC_), the _half-life_ (how long until the concentration halves, which sets how often the drug must be dosed), and the _clearance_ (how fast the body eliminates it).

ADME is governed by the molecule's physical properties — size, fattiness, charge, solubility — and is tuned throughout optimization. Two recurring failure modes show why it matters: a drug cleared too fast must be dosed impractically often, while one cleared too slowly builds up to toxic levels; and metabolism can turn a drug into a harmful breakdown product, or destroy it before it can work. For an orally taken drug, the absorption part of ADME largely determines its [[Bioavailability|bioavailability]].
