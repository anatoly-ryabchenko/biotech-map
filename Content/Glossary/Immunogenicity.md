_Immunogenicity_ is the tendency of a drug — especially a protein drug — to provoke the patient's own immune system to react against it. The immune system is built to attack things it reads as foreign, and a large, complex molecule like a therapeutic protein can look foreign enough to trigger that response, with the body producing _anti-drug antibodies_ against the medicine itself.

This is a problem in several ways. Anti-drug antibodies can _neutralize_ the drug, binding it so it can no longer act, so the treatment simply stops working. They can speed the drug's _clearance_, shortening how long each dose lasts. And the immune reaction can itself cause harm, from infusion reactions up to, rarely, dangerous responses — most seriously when antibodies raised against a protein drug cross-react with the patient's own version of that protein.

How immunogenic a protein is depends largely on how foreign it looks. An antibody taken straight from a mouse is highly immunogenic in people, which is why such antibodies are _humanized_ — re-engineered so the bulk of the molecule reads as human — while antibodies from human sources provoke far less. Other features raise the risk too: _aggregates_ (clumps of protein) are strongly immunogenic, as are certain sequence features and some non-human sugar patterns. Reducing immunogenicity — by humanizing, removing aggregation-prone and immune-triggering spots, and engineering the molecule to look as "self" as possible — is a routine goal of protein-drug design, and it is watched throughout development because it can only be confirmed fully once the drug is given to humans.
