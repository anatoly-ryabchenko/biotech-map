_Fragment-based drug discovery_ starts the search for a drug from unusually small molecules — _fragments_ of about 100–300 daltons, far smaller than a finished drug. The logic is statistical: the number of possible molecules explodes with size, so a small, well-chosen library of a few thousand fragments samples chemical space far more efficiently than a same-sized library of large molecules, and a simple fragment is more likely to find _some_ complementary spot on the target.

The price is that a fragment, making only a few contacts, binds very weakly — too weakly for an ordinary [[Assay|assay]] to register. So binding is detected by sensitive biophysical methods that can see a faint interaction directly: nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR), surface plasmon resonance, or X-ray crystallography, which can even show exactly where on the target the fragment sits. That structural picture is the real payoff.

A fragment hit is not a drug but a beachhead. Knowing precisely how and where the fragment binds, chemists _grow_ it — adding chemical groups that reach into neighbouring pockets — or _link_ two fragments binding adjacent sites into one larger, far tighter molecule. Because every atom added is guided by structure, the resulting compounds tend to be efficient binders with little wasted bulk, and the [[Structure-activity relationship|structure–activity relationship]] is read straight off the structures. The approach was introduced as "SAR by NMR" by Shuker and Fesik in 1996 and has produced approved drugs.
