_Metagenomics_ is the study of a whole microbial community by sequencing all the DNA in a sample at once — for instance everything in a scoop of gut contents — rather than isolating and growing one organism at a time. The name captures the idea: not one genome, but the combined genomes of an entire community.

Its power comes from sidestepping the need to _culture_ microbes. The vast majority of bacteria in the gut and most other environments cannot be grown in the lab — they depend on conditions and partners that are hard to reproduce — so for a century they were essentially invisible to study. Metagenomics extracts and sequences the DNA directly from the sample, then uses computers to sort the jumble of fragments by organism and gene, revealing _which_ microbes are present and in what proportions, and _what they can collectively do_ (which metabolic genes they carry), without growing anything.

In drug development this is the starting point for microbiome medicines. By comparing the gut communities of healthy people with those of patients, metagenomics pinpoints which microbes or which microbial functions are depleted or overabundant in a disease — generating the hypothesis for which organisms a live biotherapeutic should restore. Its limit is that it tells you what genes are _present_, not necessarily which are switched on or what the organisms are actually doing in the living gut, and it identifies candidates that may still prove impossible to grow into an actual product.
