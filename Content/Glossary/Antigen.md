An _antigen_ is anything the immune system recognizes as a distinct molecular target and can mount a response against — in practice, usually a protein or piece of one from a pathogen (a virus, a bacterium) or, in cancer, from a tumour cell. It is the "wanted poster" the immune system learns: show the body the right antigen, and it builds antibodies and immune cells able to recognize and attack anything carrying it.

This is the basis of vaccines. A vaccine's job is to present an antigen from a threat — most often one of the proteins studding a virus's surface — to the immune system in a safe form, so the body learns to recognize it _before_ ever meeting the real pathogen. If the real thing later appears, the primed immune system destroys it fast, and because the immune system remembers through long-lived memory cells, that protection can last for years.

Not every antigen makes a good vaccine. The immune system actually recognizes specific small regions on the antigen's surface, called [[Epitope|epitopes]], and what matters is whether the antibodies raised against those regions can actually _neutralize_ the pathogen — block it from infecting — rather than merely bind it harmlessly. A good vaccine antigen displays the right neutralizing epitopes in the right shape; a poorly chosen or wrongly shaped antigen can raise plenty of antibodies that do not protect. (Separately, a drug can act as an unwanted antigen when the body's immune system attacks the medicine itself — see [[Immunogenicity|immunogenicity]].)
