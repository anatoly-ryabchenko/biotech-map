_Adeno-associated virus_ (AAV) is a small, harmless virus that has become the workhorse vehicle for delivering genes into the body. Stripped of its own genes and repurposed, it is the dominant tool for _in-body_ gene therapy — injected into the bloodstream or a tissue to carry a therapeutic gene into the patient's own cells.

AAV is favoured for several reasons. It does not normally splice its cargo into the cell's chromosomes — the delivered gene mostly sits separately inside the nucleus — which lowers (though does not eliminate) the risk of disrupting the patient's own genes. It causes no known disease and is generally well tolerated. And it comes in many natural and engineered _serotypes_ — variants of the protein shell (capsid) that prefer different tissues — so it can be aimed, to a degree, at liver, muscle, eye, or nervous system; engineering the capsid to home to a chosen tissue and to evade pre-existing immunity is a major focus of the field.

Its defining limitation is _size_: the AAV shell holds only about 4,700 DNA letters in total, for the therapeutic gene plus all of its control sequences. Many genes simply do not fit, forcing tight trimming or splitting a gene across two vectors. Other challenges are that manufacturing produces many _empty shells_ that carry no gene and add to the immune burden; that the very high doses needed for whole-body delivery can trigger dangerous immune reactions and liver injury — in 2025 two boys died of acute liver failure after a high-dose AAV gene therapy for Duchenne muscular dystrophy, leading US regulators to add their strongest (boxed) warning and restrict it to patients still able to walk; and that many people already carry antibodies against common AAVs that would neutralize the vector. Several AAV gene therapies are now approved.
