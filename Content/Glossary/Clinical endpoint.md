A _clinical endpoint_ is the specific, measured outcome a clinical trial uses to decide whether a treatment worked — chosen and defined _before_ the trial begins, so the result cannot be reinterpreted afterward to suit the answer. It is the trial's definition of success, and choosing it well is central to producing evidence a regulator will accept.

The strongest kind is a _clinical outcome_ that matters directly to patients: living longer, not having a heart attack, recovering from an infection. But such outcomes can take years and huge numbers of patients to observe, so trials often use a _surrogate endpoint_ instead — a quicker, measurable stand-in believed to predict the real benefit, such as lowering blood pressure (standing in for preventing strokes) or shrinking a tumour (for living longer). Surrogates speed trials up but carry a risk: a drug can move the surrogate without delivering the real benefit, so a surrogate is trustworthy only when it is well established to track the outcome that matters. In vaccines the equivalent is a _correlate of protection_ — an antibody level shown to predict immunity — which lets a trial rely on a blood measurement rather than waiting for natural infections.

The choice of endpoint shapes everything: the trial's size, length, and cost, and whether its result will convince regulators. A regulator may grant an _accelerated approval_ on a surrogate endpoint for a serious disease, then require confirmation on a true clinical outcome afterward. A trial that hits a poorly chosen endpoint can look successful yet fail to show the medicine actually helps anyone.
