_Druggability_ is whether a target — usually a protein — can actually be acted on by a drug at all. A target may sit at the very centre of a disease yet be impossible to drug, and judging this up front is part of choosing what to pursue.

For the commonest kind of drug, a small molecule, druggability comes down to the target's surface offering a suitable _pocket_: a well-formed cavity of the right size, shape, and chemistry, where a small molecule can settle and bind tightly (a binding event of the kind described in [[Docking|docking]]). Enzymes and receptors often have such pockets — the natural clefts where their own substrates or signals bind — and tend to be highly druggable. The hard cases are proteins with flat, featureless, or shallow surfaces, such as those that act by sticking to other proteins across a broad contact: there is nowhere for a small molecule to grip, and these have long been called "undruggable."

Crucially, druggability depends on the _kind_ of drug. A target with no small-molecule pocket may still be reachable another way — blocked by an antibody if it sits on the cell surface, dragged to destruction by a protein degrader, or silenced at the genetic level before the protein is ever made — so the rise of new modalities keeps expanding what counts as druggable. Hopkins and Groom's 2002 analysis of the "druggable genome" estimated that only a minority of human proteins are readily druggable by small molecules, a key reason the choice of target and the choice of modality are made together.
