A natural strand of RNA or DNA makes a hopeless drug: enzymes in the blood and inside cells, called _nucleases_, chop it apart within minutes, and a bare strand is cleared fast and barely taken up by cells. _Oligonucleotide chemical modifications_ are the chemical changes made to the strand's building blocks to fix this — turning a fragile genetic strand into a durable medicine while keeping it able to recognize its target message by base-pairing.

Two parts of the strand are altered. The _backbone_ — the chain of phosphate links holding the letters together — is the favourite target of nucleases, so it is changed, most commonly to a _phosphorothioate_ link (one oxygen swapped for sulfur), which resists the enzymes and also helps the strand stick to blood proteins and slip into cells. The _sugar_ on each letter is modified too — common versions are _2′-MOE_ and _locked nucleic acid_ (LNA) — which makes the strand bind its target more tightly and further blocks nuclease attack.

The art is that these modifications must be _placed_ carefully, not used everywhere, because some of the cell's own machinery the drug relies on needs natural-like chemistry to function. A gene-silencing "gapmer" antisense oligonucleotide, for instance, keeps a central window of unmodified DNA-like letters so the enzyme RNase H will still cut the bound message, with modifications only on the flanks; an siRNA must stay compatible with the [[RNA interference|RNA-interference]] machinery. So a real oligonucleotide drug is a precise mosaic of modified and natural-like positions, balancing stability, binding strength, target-cutting, and safety — the central design problem of the modality.
