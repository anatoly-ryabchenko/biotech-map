_CRISPR_ is a tool for cutting or editing DNA at a chosen spot, adapted from a defense system bacteria use against viruses. Its power is that it can be aimed at almost any sequence simply by supplying a matching guide — which is what turned genome editing from painstaking, one-gene-at-a-time engineering into something routine.

The system has two parts. The first is a protein — most famously _Cas9_ — that can cut through both strands of DNA. On its own it cuts nothing in particular; it is aimed by the second part, a short _guide RNA_ whose letters are written to match the target DNA sequence. The guide leads the protein to the matching site, the protein checks the match, and it cuts. There is one important constraint: the protein will only cut next to a short signature sequence in the DNA called a _PAM_, so an edit can only be placed where a PAM sits nearby. Once the DNA is cut, the cell's own repair machinery takes over — hurried repair usually garbles and disables the gene, which is one way to switch a harmful gene off.

CRISPR is also the foundation for a family of more refined tools. _Base editors_ pair a disabled Cas protein (one that no longer cuts) with an enzyme that chemically rewrites a single DNA letter, and _prime editors_ add a copy-writing enzyme that pastes in a short new sequence — both achieving precise corrections without a full double-strand cut. Beyond making gene-editing medicines, CRISPR is also used to insert genes into cells (as in some cell therapies) and to knock out genes systematically in research screens. The programmable Cas9 system was described by Jinek, Doudna, and Charpentier in 2012.
