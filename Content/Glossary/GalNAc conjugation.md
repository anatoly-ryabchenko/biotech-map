_GalNAc conjugation_ is the trick that lets oligonucleotide drugs home to the liver: attaching a small cluster of the sugar _N-acetylgalactosamine_ (GalNAc) to the drug. Liver cells (hepatocytes) carry large numbers of a surface receptor — the asialoglycoprotein receptor — whose natural job is to grab molecules bearing this exact sugar and pull them inside. A drug decorated with a three-pronged (trivalent) GalNAc tag is therefore seized by liver cells and taken up efficiently, while other tissues, which lack the receptor, largely ignore it.

This solves the central problem of oligonucleotide delivery for one important organ. Because the targeting is so efficient, a GalNAc-conjugated oligonucleotide can be given as a simple _injection under the skin_ (subcutaneously), from which it drains into the blood and concentrates in the liver — no infusion, and no wrapping in a particle needed. The effect is potent and long-lasting: a single dose can silence a target gene in the liver for months, letting some drugs be given just a few times a year.

GalNAc conjugation is now the dominant delivery approach for liver-targeted oligonucleotides — including gene-silencing siRNAs that work through [[RNA interference|RNA interference]] — and lies behind a series of approved drugs. Its great limitation is that it works for the liver and almost nowhere else: no equally clean "doorway" receptor has been found for most other tissues, which is why reaching muscle, brain, or other organs remains the field's central delivery challenge. The approach was established for siRNA by Nair and colleagues in 2014.
