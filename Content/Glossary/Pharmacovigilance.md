_Pharmacovigilance_ is the ongoing watch over a medicine's safety after it is approved and in wide use — systematically collecting, analyzing, and acting on reports of harm to catch problems the clinical trials could not. It is the safety net that runs for as long as a drug is on the market.

It exists because clinical trials, however rigorous, are limited: a trial enrols at most a few thousand carefully selected patients, followed for a defined period. Once a drug is approved it is taken by far larger and more varied populations — older people, those with other illnesses, those on other medicines, sometimes during pregnancy — and for much longer. Effects too _rare_ to appear in a few thousand people (a serious reaction in one in ten thousand), or too _slow_ to show up in a trial's timeframe, only become visible at this scale. Pharmacovigilance gathers adverse-event reports from doctors, patients, and manufacturers into large databases, and analysts comb them for a _signal_ — a pattern suggesting the drug may be causing a harm not seen before.

When a credible signal emerges, regulators can act: updating the label with a new warning, restricting who may receive the drug, requiring further studies, or in serious cases withdrawing it from the market. This is why approval is not the end of a drug's safety story but the start of a new phase of it. When real-world surveillance uncovers a serious new risk, it can send the question back to formal clinical testing to be settled rigorously.
