A messenger RNA strand is built from four letters (A, U, G, C). _Modified nucleosides_ are chemically altered versions of those letters, used in place of the ordinary ones when making an mRNA drug — and one modification in particular is the reason mRNA medicines work at all.

The problem they solve is the immune alarm. A cell treats foreign RNA as a sign of viral infection: sensors inside the cell detect ordinary, unmodified RNA and trigger an inflammatory response that destroys the RNA and can shut the cell down — so a naive mRNA drug would be wiped out before making much protein, while causing unwanted inflammation. The breakthrough was the discovery that swapping the letter _uridine_ for a modified version — especially _N1-methylpseudouridine_ — lets the strand be read and translated normally by the protein-building machinery while slipping past those sensors, so it is not flagged as foreign. The result is far more protein made, with far less immune reaction.

Building the entire strand from the modified letter is what made mRNA vaccines and therapies practical, and the modified letters are incorporated when the strand is manufactured by [[In vitro transcription|in vitro transcription]]. How much immune quieting is wanted depends on the use: a protein-replacement mRNA wants maximum silence, while a vaccine actually benefits from a controlled amount of immune stimulation. The discovery that nucleoside modification suppresses RNA's immune recognition, by Karikó and Weissman in 2005, underpinned the mRNA vaccines and was recognized with the 2023 Nobel Prize.
