_Solid-phase peptide synthesis_ (SPPS) is the standard way to build a peptide — a short chain of amino acids — by chemistry, assembling it one residue at a time while it stays anchored to a solid support. That anchoring is the key trick: because the growing chain is tethered to an insoluble resin bead, excess reagents and byproducts can simply be washed away after each step, instead of having to purify a product out of solution every time.

The chain is built in a repeating cycle. The first amino acid is attached to tiny resin beads. Each new amino acid is added with its reactive groups _protected_ (chemically capped) except the one meant to join the chain, so it links only where intended; then the cap on the newly added residue is stripped off (_deprotection_) to expose the next attachment point, the beads are washed, and the next residue is coupled. Repeating couple–deprotect–wash extends the chain residue by residue, in the chosen sequence. When the full chain is built, the peptide is _cleaved_ off the resin and its remaining protecting groups removed.

The method's signature weakness is arithmetic. Each coupling is efficient but not perfect — a small fraction of chains fail to add a residue every cycle — and over the dozens of cycles a long peptide needs, these failures accumulate into a population of slightly-wrong chains (missing a residue here or there) that are chemically very similar to the correct product and must be purified away. This, together with the large volumes of solvent each cycle consumes, is why long peptides are costly to make. Introduced by Merrifield in 1963, SPPS is used from the first lab synthesis through commercial manufacturing.
