_Insertional mutagenesis_ is the danger that arises when a gene-delivery vector splices its cargo into the patient's DNA at the wrong place — and, by landing in or near one of the genes that control cell growth, accidentally switches that gene on or breaks it, nudging the cell toward becoming a cancer. It is the signature long-term risk of any genetic medicine that _integrates_ into the genome.

The mechanism is concrete. Vectors that integrate — chiefly [[Lentiviral vector|lentiviral]] and the older retroviral vectors — insert more or less semi-randomly across the genome's three billion letters. Usually this is harmless, but if the insertion lands next to a _proto-oncogene_ (a normal gene that drives cell division and that turns cancer-causing when over-active), the vector's own built-in "switch-on" sequences can force that gene to be read far too much, releasing the brakes on the cell's division. A single such event, in a single cell, can seed a tumour — and because it is written permanently into the DNA, it cannot be undone, and the cancer may take years to surface.

This is not hypothetical: in early gene-therapy trials for an inherited immune deficiency, several children were cured but later developed leukaemia, traced to the vector having switched on a growth-controlling gene called LMO2. That experience reshaped the field — driving the design of safer vectors that insert in less dangerous places, careful monitoring for any cell clone that grows to dominate, and the mandatory years-long follow-up that integrating genetic therapies now carry. It is a central concern of advanced-therapy safety testing.
