Negative design is the deliberate goal of making a molecule *not* act on things it is not meant to act on. When a protein or small molecule is designed as a drug, the obvious objective is that it binds and acts on its intended target — this is *positive design*, optimizing for the wanted interaction. But a molecule loose in the body meets thousands of others, and any of them it also binds is an *off-target*. Negative design is the other half of the job: actively designing so the molecule leaves those off-targets alone. Positive design pulls the molecule toward its target; negative design pushes it away from everything else; the combination is what produces *selectivity* (also called specificity) — acting on one thing and not the rest.

This matters because off-target binding is a major source of side effects and toxicity. A drug that also sticks to an unintended protein can disrupt whatever that protein does, and such effects are hard to foresee. Selectivity is hardest to achieve precisely when a non-target closely resembles the target: a change that makes the molecule bind its target better tends to make it bind a look-alike better too.

The off-targets a design is steered away from are not picked at random — they are the molecules most likely to be mistaken for the target. In practice these are chosen as the target's *close structural relatives*: other members of the same protein family (paralogs), the same protein from related species (homologs), and any protein already known to bind promiscuously or to have caused off-target trouble for similar drugs. These specific structures are gathered into an explicit set of *non-targets* — sometimes called decoys or *negative states* — and handed to the design process alongside the intended target. The design is then optimized to bind the target while being *penalized* for binding any of the non-targets; weighing wanted and unwanted complexes together this way is called multistate (or negative-state) design. One way the result is checked is by [[docking]] the candidate against both the target and each non-target: a good design is predicted to bind the target and to fail to bind the decoys. Because judging binding depends on knowing the molecules' shapes, these checks lean on [[structure-prediction networks]] to fold the candidate together with each target and non-target before scoring the interaction.
