A _chelator_ is a molecule that wraps around a metal atom and grips it tightly, like a claw closing around it — the name comes from the Greek for "claw." It holds the metal by clamping onto it at several points at once, which makes the resulting cage far more stable than a single attachment would be.

In radiopharmaceuticals the chelator is the essential connector. These drugs deliver a radioactive _metal_ atom to diseased cells, but a bare radioactive metal cannot be aimed — so a chelator is attached to the targeting molecule (the vector that homes to the tumour) and then loaded with the radioactive metal, locking it onto the vector. The chelator is what lets one targeting molecule carry whichever radioactive metal is wanted.

Its critical property is that it must _never let go_ inside the body. If the chelator releases its radioactive metal into the circulation, that loose radioactive atom drifts off and lodges in the wrong place — often bone or kidney — irradiating healthy tissue far from the tumour. So the match between chelator and metal matters intensely: different radioactive metals have different sizes and chemistries, and each needs a chelator shaped to hold it stably under the conditions inside the body. Choosing or designing the right chelator for a given metal — one that loads quickly and holds permanently — is a core part of building a radiopharmaceutical, and the same chelator design enables the [[Theranostics|theranostic]] pairing of an imaging metal and a therapeutic one on a single vector.
