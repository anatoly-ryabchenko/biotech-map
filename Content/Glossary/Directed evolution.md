_Directed evolution_ is a way to improve a biological molecule — a protein, an enzyme, a virus shell — by mimicking natural evolution in the lab: make many random variants, keep the ones that perform best, and repeat, round after round, until the molecule has the wanted property. It is how the field improves things it cannot yet design from scratch.

Each round has two parts. First, _diversity_: a large library of variants is generated — by introducing random mutations into the molecule's gene, or by shuffling pieces of related genes together — so thousands to millions of slightly different versions exist. Second, _selection_ (or screening): the variants are put under a test that favours the desired property — binding a target more tightly, surviving heat, entering a particular cell type — and only the winners are kept. Their genes go into the next round for more diversification and selection, so improvements accumulate step by step, exactly as natural selection accumulates beneficial changes over generations — but aimed at a goal the experimenter sets and compressed into weeks.

The power of the approach is that it needs no understanding of _why_ a change helps — it selects on outcomes — so it can optimize properties too complex to predict or design. It is used to boost the potency of a weak designed protein, to mature an enzyme into a useful catalyst, and to evolve a viral-vector shell that homes to a chosen tissue. Its limits are that you can only get what you can _select_ for — the test must truly capture the goal — and what your starting library contains. Frances Arnold's development of directed evolution of enzymes was recognized with the 2018 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
