An antibody is a Y-shaped protein the immune system makes to recognize one specific target. The two tips of the Y carry the binding site — a surface shaped to grip a particular spot (an [[Epitope|epitope]]) on the target — while the stem of the Y is the constant part the rest of the immune system reads. A _monoclonal_ antibody is one single, exactly-defined antibody: every molecule is identical, with the same sequence and the same binding site, produced from one clone of cells so that each batch behaves the same.

This matters because the natural immune response is _poly_clonal — a swarm of many different antibodies against the same target, impossible to reproduce as a consistent drug. A monoclonal antibody isolates and immortalizes the production of just one, giving a reproducible molecule of known specificity. That uniformity is what makes antibodies usable as medicines: precise targeting of one protein and little else, batch after batch.

Monoclonal antibodies underlie a huge class of drugs — they can block a signalling protein, flag a cancer cell for immune attack, or neutralize a toxin. They are also the source of parts used by other modalities: the antibody in an antibody–drug conjugate, and the _scFv_ (the two binding tips fused into one small unit) that forms the targeting end of a chimeric antigen receptor in cell therapy. The first way to make them — fusing an antibody-producing cell with an immortal tumour cell to create a _hybridoma_ — was reported by Köhler and Milstein in 1975.
