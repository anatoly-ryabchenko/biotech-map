A protein is made by a living cell reading the gene that codes for it. *Protein expression* is that act of production — a cell takes the gene, a stretch of DNA spelling out the protein's amino-acid sequence, and turns it into the actual protein. Saying a protein "is expressed" means a cell has built it; saying a design "fails to express" means the cell did not produce usable amounts of it.

The production runs in two stages. First, *transcription*: the cell copies the gene's DNA into a working messenger molecule called mRNA, a transcript carrying the same sequence in a portable form. Second, *translation*: molecular machines called ribosomes read the mRNA three letters at a time and join amino acids in the order it specifies, growing the chain that then folds into the finished protein (the folding itself follows [[protein folding principles]]). This DNA-to-mRNA-to-protein flow is the basic route by which any cell makes any of its proteins.

To make a *chosen* protein — a drug, an enzyme, a designed protein — its gene is placed into a *host* cell and the host's own machinery is borrowed to run transcription and translation. The gene is carried in on an *expression vector*: a piece of DNA built to hold the gene together with the control signals a cell needs to switch it on. The host cells are then grown in culture, produce the protein, and the protein is harvested and purified out.

Which host is used is a real decision, not a detail. Bacteria such as E. coli grow fast and cheaply and are the default for simple proteins, but they cannot perform some of the folding assistance and chemical decoration — for instance attaching sugar groups — that many human proteins require, so a human protein made in bacteria may emerge misfolded or inactive. Mammalian, yeast, and insect cells can carry out that folding and decoration and so are used for complex human proteins, at higher cost and slower pace.

Expression is also where designs frequently fail, which is why it is treated as a first hard test rather than a formality. A cell may produce none of the protein at all; or produce it but have it misfold and clump into insoluble, useless aggregates; or produce too little to work with. The cause may be that the protein is intrinsically hard for that host to fold, or that the gene's sequence is awkward for the host to translate efficiently. These failures are common enough that predicting whether a protein will express, and choosing and tuning the host to make it, are explicit steps before a designed protein is ever built physically.

Essentially every protein-based medicine — antibodies, enzymes, hormones, designed proteins — must be expressed by living cells before it can be tested or sold, so expression is the gate that turns a sequence on a screen into a physical molecule, and one of the major bottlenecks and failure points in development. The same process also runs inside patients: gene and cell therapies work by delivering a gene so that the patient's own cells express the therapeutic protein.
