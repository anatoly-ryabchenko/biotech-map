_High-throughput screening_ (HTS) is the industrial-scale hunt for a starting compound: physically testing a very large collection of molecules — a _library_ of hundreds of thousands to a few million compounds — against a target to see which ones do anything at all. It is brute-force search, made practical by automation.

The library is stored as tiny amounts of each compound in _microplates_: flat trays carrying hundreds or thousands of miniature wells, one compound per well. Robots dispense the target and reagents into every well, and an [[Assay|assay]] with a fast optical readout — usually light or colour — is measured across all the wells at once by a plate reader. This way a single facility can test an entire library in days to weeks, each well a separate miniature experiment.

What comes out are _hits_ — wells where the readout moved enough to suggest the compound acts on the target. Hit rates are low, commonly well under 1%, and a large share of apparent hits are artifacts (compounds that interfere with the readout, clump, or behave non-specifically), so every hit is re-tested and confirmed before it is trusted. HTS does not produce drugs; it produces starting points, which are then confirmed, grouped into chemical families, and handed to optimization.

The strength of HTS is breadth without preconception — it can turn up chemistry no one would have predicted. Its limits are that you can only find what the library contains, and that the artifacts masquerading as hits must constantly be weeded out.
