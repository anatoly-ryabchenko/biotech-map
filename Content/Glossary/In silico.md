*In silico* means done by computer — the work is carried out as a calculation, model, or simulation rather than with physical material. It is the counterpart to two older terms: *in vitro*, done in a test tube or dish with real molecules outside a living thing, and *in vivo*, done inside a living organism. Where those handle real matter, in silico work runs a numerical stand-in for that matter inside a machine.

It matters in drug development because of cost and speed. Making a molecule, producing a protein, or running a lab assay is slow and expensive, so the field puts heavy effort into predicting an experiment's outcome on a computer first and carrying only the most promising candidates to the bench. A single in silico run can screen thousands of designs for the time and money it would take to test a handful physically. The catch is that an in silico result is only as trustworthy as the model behind it: a confidently scored prediction can still be wrong, so in silico work is used to narrow and rank candidates, not to replace the physical experiment that ultimately confirms them.
