A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds up into a particular three-dimensional shape. The *backbone* is the part of that shape you get if you ignore which amino acid sits at each position and keep only the repeating chain of atoms that every amino acid shares — the continuous thread that runs through the whole protein and traces out its overall fold. It is the geometric scaffold of the protein, sequence aside: the path the chain takes through space, the helices and sheets it forms, and how they pack together, but not yet the chemical identity of each link.

A backbone-generation network is a machine-learning model that invents new backbones — new 3D scaffolds that no natural protein necessarily has. Used in [[in silico]] protein design, it is given a target (the molecule the new protein should act on) and the *hotspot residues* — the specific patch of the target the new protein should make contact with — and it proposes backbone shapes predicted to sit against the target in a useful way (see [[docking]] for what binding usefully means). The desired interaction is decided upstream; the network's task is to find a physical form that can carry it out.

The leading models work by a method called *diffusion*, which is easiest to understand starting from the idea of *noise* — small random jitters added to the position of every atom. Training proceeds in two directions. First, the forward direction, which is only used to create training examples: take a real protein structure from a database and repeatedly add a little noise to its atoms, step by step, blurring the shape until after many steps it is a formless random cloud of points. The model is shown these progressively noisier versions and trained on the reverse direction: at each step, estimate the noise that was just added — equivalently, take one small step back toward the cleaner structure. After enough examples it learns, for any partly noisy arrangement, how to nudge it slightly toward something more protein-like.

Generating a brand-new backbone then runs that learned reverse step on its own. The model starts from a completely random cloud — pure noise, no structure at all — and applies its "remove a little noise" step over and over. Across many steps a coherent fold emerges out of the randomness, never copied from any single training protein but consistent with the patterns the model learned from all of them. The useful part is that this denoising can be *steered*: at each step the model is biased so the fold taking shape places its contacts against the requested hotspots, producing a backbone built to meet that specific target. RFdiffusion (Baker Lab, 2023) is the best-known example.

A central requirement is *designability*. A proposed backbone is only useful if some real amino-acid chain will reliably fold into it and hold steady there; many geometrically plausible shapes have no sequence that settles into them. A backbone-generation network can judge this only loosely on its own, so designs are checked downstream — proposing a sequence for the backbone with [[inverse-folding networks]], then forecasting where that sequence actually folds with [[structure-prediction networks]]. When no sequence is predicted to fold into the proposed shape, the backbone is undesignable and the work loops back for a new one. Even strong models produce a meaningful fraction of undesignable backbones, which is why this checking matters.

Standard backbone generation reasons about protein atoms alone. But blockers that must wrap a small molecule, enzymes that must cradle a [[Substrate|substrate]], and switches that bind a [[Cofactor|cofactor]] all need the backbone shaped *around* a non-protein partner. *All-atom* backbone-generation networks handle this: they place the new protein's backbone around the explicit atoms of a small molecule, cofactor, or substrate rather than around protein hotspots only — necessary for designing enzymes and conformational modulators.

Building and testing real proteins in a lab is slow and costly, so the industry tries to invent good shapes on the computer first. Backbone generation is the first of those computational steps, and the quality and designability of what it proposes sets a ceiling on everything that follows.
