_Good Manufacturing Practice_ (GMP) is the binding system of rules that governs how medicines are made, designed to guarantee that every batch is safe, pure, and consistent — that the drug a patient receives is exactly what it is supposed to be, every time. It is not a single technique but a whole regime of requirements, enforced by regulators, that a facility must follow to be allowed to make a drug.

In practice GMP means everything is controlled and written down. Every raw material is tested and traced; every piece of equipment is qualified and cleaned to documented standards; every step is carried out to a pre-approved written procedure and recorded as it happens, so there is a complete paper trail for each batch. Staff are trained and their actions logged; the facility's air, water, and surfaces are monitored for contamination; and every finished batch is tested against fixed specifications before it can be released. The guiding principle is that quality cannot be _tested into_ a product at the end — it must be _built in_ by controlling the whole process, because for many medicines you cannot fully verify a batch is safe just by examining the final vials.

GMP is why making the clinical and commercial supply of a drug is so much more demanding than making the same molecule once in a research lab. Regulators inspect factories in person before approval and periodically afterward, and a serious GMP failure can block an approval or force a product off the market — because, as regulators put it, a drug is only as safe as the batches actually produced. Every modality, from a simple pill to a living cell therapy, must be made under GMP.
