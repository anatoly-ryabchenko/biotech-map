Lead discovery answers the next question after a target is chosen: **what actual thing can we get that acts on this target?** That thing differs by modality — a small molecule, an antibody, a designed protein, a peptide, a genetic construct, engineered cells, a virus, or a set of microbial strains — but the job is the same everywhere: produce a first real candidate that demonstrably engages the target.

This is the stage where the shared path forks into modality-specific work, so each modality has its own discovery process. The methods look very different from one another. For a small molecule, enormous chemical libraries are screened against the target to find a starting point that touches it — a "hit". For an antibody, a living immune system or a laboratory library is used to fish out proteins that latch onto the target and nothing else. For a designed protein, a candidate is built from scratch on a computer. For gene editing, a guide sequence and an editing protein are designed to aim at one exact spot in the genome. For a cell therapy, an artificial receptor is designed and a cell source chosen. For a microbiome medicine, the responsible microbes are identified and isolated. What unites them is that the output is a **first candidate, not a finished one**: it engages the target but is typically too weak, too unselective, too unstable, or too unrefined to be a drug yet.

The stage is done when there are one or more promising but still unoptimized candidates — a hit, a set of binders, an initial construct, candidate strains — ready to be refined.
