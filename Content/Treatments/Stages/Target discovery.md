Target discovery is where every project starts, and it answers one question: **what in the body should a medicine act on to change this disease?** That thing is the target. A project enters this stage with a disease and the unmet need behind it, and leaves it with a chosen target and a decision about what kind of medicine to build.

A target is usually a specific molecule — most often a protein — that is doing too much, too little, or the wrong thing in a disease. But "target" means different things for different kinds of medicine: a faulty gene to supply a working copy of, an exact spot in the genome to correct, a marker on the surface of diseased cells, a piece of a germ the immune system should learn (a vaccine's antigen), or a microbial function that has gone missing. Choosing the target is the single most important decision in the whole project, because everything downstream is built to act on this one thing; if the choice is wrong, no cleverness later can rescue it.

The work is to build a credible, evidence-backed case that acting on the target will actually help patients — drawing on human genetics, on cell and animal studies where the target is switched off, and on measurements from patient tissue, and wanting them all to point the same way. The stage does not end in certainty. It ends in a reasoned bet, paired with a second decision that is just as consequential: **which kind of medicine is best suited to act on this particular target.** That modality decision is what sends the project down one of the pipeline branches that follow.

The stage is done when the project has both a validated target and a decision about which kind of medicine is best suited to act on it. It is also the one stage every kind of medicine shares as a single step, before each follows its own path.
