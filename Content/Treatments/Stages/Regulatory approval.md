Regulatory approval answers the question: **has this medicine proven, to an independent public regulator, that its benefits outweigh its risks — and on exactly what terms may it be used?** A drug that has passed its trials still cannot be sold. A government body whose job is to protect the public must first review all the evidence and grant permission. Every modality passes through here.

The work is to assemble everything the project has learned into one enormous submission and then to work with the regulator toward a yes or no. The submission pulls together three strands: the benefit-and-safety results of the human trials; the underlying science that justified testing in people; and a detailed account of how the drug is made and kept consistent and pure batch after batch — regulators care deeply about manufacturing, because a drug is only as safe as the batches actually produced. Review is a months-long back-and-forth of detailed questions, requested analyses, and sometimes in-person inspections of the factories. A central output is the label — the negotiated, official statement of which patients the drug is approved for, at what dose, with what warnings, for what condition. Some modalities also pass through a specialized route layered on top of the general one: living and genetic medicines through a pathway that demands unusually strong evidence of manufacturing consistency and builds in mandatory long-term follow-up, and radiopharmaceuticals through an added layer governing the safe handling of radioactive material.

The stage is done when the regulator grants a **marketing authorization with an agreed label** — legal permission to make and sell the drug for a defined use.
