Preclinical studies answer the question that must be settled before any person receives the candidate: **does it actually work, is it safe enough to try in humans, and what is the safest dose to start them on?** Everything so far has been about making a good candidate; this stage is the first hard test of whether that candidate does what was hoped in a living system rather than on a screen or a bench.

The work runs from simpler systems to more complete ones. First comes testing in cells grown in dishes — confirming the candidate engages its target inside a real, crowded cell, that engaging it produces the intended biological effect, and that it does not also hit things it shouldn't. Then comes testing in living animals: giving the candidate to animals in which the disease (or something resembling it) has been induced, to see whether the disease actually eases, and at the same time tracking how the body absorbs, distributes, breaks down, and clears the drug — so the dose and dosing schedule can be worked out. Finally comes formal, audited animal safety testing under a strict quality system, designed to find every adverse effect and, from the highest harmless dose, to set a safe first dose for humans. Layered on top are safety questions specific to certain modalities — the cancer, genome-insertion, and uncontrolled-spread risks of living and genetic medicines; the payload risks of conjugated biologics; a vaccine's ability to provoke protection; a microbiome product's ability to take hold; a radiopharmaceutical's radiation dose to healthy organs.

The stage is done when there is **evidence the candidate works in a living body, an acceptable safety profile, and a defined safe human starting dose** — together, the right to be tested in people.
