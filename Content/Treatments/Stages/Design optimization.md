Design optimization answers the question: **can we turn this rough first candidate into a single, well-behaved one good enough to test in animals — and, for living and genetic medicines, actually build it?** A candidate from discovery proves only that something can act on the target. It is rarely usable as-is: it may bind too weakly, hit things it shouldn't, fall apart in storage, or not yet exist as a physical product at all.

The work takes two broad shapes depending on the modality. For molecules and biologics it is **refinement, run as a repeating loop** — change part of the candidate, test it, let the result guide the next change, and turn the loop again. A small molecule is reshaped for stronger, more selective binding and for being handled well by the body; an antibody is strengthened and made human-like so the immune system tolerates it; a designed protein is built for real and measured against its prediction; an editing guide is tuned so on-target edits work and off-target ones become vanishingly rare. Several of these processes loop back to discovery when refinement hits a wall — this stage is where a candidate is often sent back to the drawing board. For living and genetic medicines the stage also **physically constructs the medicine**: a viral carrier is engineered, immune cells are engineered and grown into useful numbers, a payload is linked to its antibody. Throughout, many goals are pushed at once — strength, selectivity, stability, and whether the thing can actually be manufactured.

The stage is done when a **single optimized, validated candidate** has emerged — strong, selective, stable, makeable, and, for cell, gene, and viral medicines, actually built — ready for safety and efficacy testing.
