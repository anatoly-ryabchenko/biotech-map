A **peptide** is a short chain of amino acids — the same building blocks proteins are made of, but only a handful to a few dozen long. That size sits deliberately between a small molecule and a full protein: large enough to grip a broad, flat target surface (the protein-to-protein contacts small molecules struggle with), yet small enough to be built by chemistry rather than grown in living cells. Many of the body's own signalling molecules are peptides — insulin, the gut hormone GLP-1 — which gives this modality a rich set of natural starting points.

Mechanistically a peptide usually works by engaging a receptor or binding partner on a cell's surface, mimicking or blocking a natural signal. Its characteristic weakness is fragility: a plain peptide chain is chopped up by the body's enzymes within minutes and cleared by the kidneys, so the pipeline is largely about engineering stability (cyclization, chemical staples, fatty-acid tails that extend its life) into a molecule that still binds.
