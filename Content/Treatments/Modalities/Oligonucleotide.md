An **oligonucleotide** drug is a short, lab-made strand of genetic letters (about 15–25 long) designed to recognize one specific messenger RNA — the working copy of a gene — by the same base-pairing rule that holds DNA's two strands together. Because the drug is chosen simply by reading off the complement of a target gene's sequence, almost any gene's message is in principle addressable, including disease proteins with no druggable pocket.

There are two mechanisms, carried through the pipeline as variants. _Antisense oligonucleotides_ are single strands that either trigger an enzyme to cut the target message (silencing the gene) or mask a signal to redirect how the message is assembled. _Small interfering RNAs_ are double strands that load into the cell's natural [[RNA interference|RNA-interference]] machinery to find and cut the matching message. The modality's defining challenges are chemistry — heavy [[Oligonucleotide chemical modifications|chemical modification]] is needed so the strand survives the body — and delivery, which today reaches the liver well (via [[GalNAc conjugation|GalNAc]] targeting) but most other tissues poorly.
