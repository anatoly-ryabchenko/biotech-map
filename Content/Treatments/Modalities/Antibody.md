An **antibody** drug is a [[Monoclonal antibody|monoclonal antibody]] — a Y-shaped protein, identical in every copy, whose two tips are shaped to grip one precise spot on a target. That natural precision is what makes antibodies such valuable medicines: they can stick to a chosen protein and almost nothing else. They are large proteins given by injection, and because the body recycles them, they can last weeks per dose.

Antibodies act in several ways: blocking a signalling protein by binding it, flagging a diseased cell so the immune system destroys it, or neutralizing a toxin or virus. The pipeline turns a first binder into a drug that is tight-binding, fully "human" (so the patient's immune system tolerates it), and well-behaved enough to manufacture and store at high concentration. Sub-variants such as bispecific antibodies (binding two targets at once) and antibody fragments are narrated within the pipeline rather than split out.
