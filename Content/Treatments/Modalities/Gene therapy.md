A **gene therapy** treats a disease caused by a missing or broken gene by delivering a working copy of that gene into the patient's cells, so the cells themselves start making the protein they could not make before. It addresses the root cause rather than the symptoms, and because the delivered gene can persist, a single treatment can in principle last for years.

The therapy is a genetic payload — the gene plus the control sequences that switch it on — packed into a delivery vehicle, almost always an [[Adeno-associated virus|adeno-associated virus]], a harmless virus repurposed to carry genes into cells. The dominant challenges run through the whole pipeline: the vehicle's tight cargo limit (many genes barely fit), aiming it at the right tissue, the immune system's reaction to the high doses needed, and the long-term risk that an inserted gene could disrupt the patient's own DNA. Being permanent and biological, gene therapy also carries mandatory years-long follow-up and a specialized regulatory route.
