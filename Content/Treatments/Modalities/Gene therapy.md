A **gene therapy** treats a disease caused by a missing or broken gene by delivering a working copy of that gene into the patient's cells, so the cells themselves start making the protein they could not make before. It addresses the root cause rather than the symptoms, and because the delivered gene can persist, a single treatment can in principle last for years.

The therapy is a genetic payload — the gene plus the control sequences that switch it on — packed into a delivery vehicle, for gene replacement almost always an [[Adeno-associated virus|adeno-associated virus]], a harmless virus repurposed to carry genes into cells. The dominant challenges run through the whole pipeline: the vehicle's tight cargo limit (many genes barely fit), aiming it at the right tissue, the immune system's reaction to the high doses needed, and the long-term risk that an inserted gene could disrupt the patient's own DNA. Being permanent and biological, gene therapy also carries mandatory years-long follow-up and a specialized regulatory route.

One variant runs through this pipeline without being a separate modality: _in-body CAR-T_ (also called in vivo CAR-T). A conventional CAR-T [[Cell therapy|cell therapy]] collects a patient's T cells, engineers them in a factory over two to four weeks, and infuses them back after a course of chemotherapy that thins out the patient's own white blood cells to make room (_lymphodepletion_). In-body CAR-T instead injects, typically into a vein, a vector carrying the gene for a [[Chimeric antigen receptor|chimeric antigen receptor]], so that the patient's own T cells are engineered where they already are. Step for step it is gene-therapy work — design a gene cassette, engineer a vector, formulate it, test it, manufacture it — with no cells collected, grown or frozen, which is why the map narrates it here as a variant and not under cell therapy. Its vehicle is usually a [[Lentiviral vector|lentiviral vector]] re-aimed at T cells rather than an AAV; some programmes use [[Lipid nanoparticle|lipid nanoparticles]] carrying mRNA (a temporary copy of the gene's instructions) instead, so the receptor is made only for a limited time. As of 2026 several programmes are in early trials, among them Kelonia's KLN-1010 (for multiple myeloma; Kelonia agreed in April 2026 to be acquired by Eli Lilly) and Legend Biotech's LB2501 (for lymphoma).
