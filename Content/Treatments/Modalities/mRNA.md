An **mRNA** drug is itself a messenger RNA — the instruction strand a cell reads to build a protein — delivered so that the patient's own cells manufacture a desired protein for a while. That protein might be a viral or tumour antigen (an mRNA vaccine, teaching the immune system to recognize it) or a missing functional protein (a replacement therapy). Rather than supplying a finished drug molecule, mRNA turns the body into the factory.

The medicine works only because of two enabling tricks, both carried through the pipeline: building the strand from [[Modified nucleosides|modified nucleosides]] so the cell does not destroy it as foreign, and wrapping it in a [[Lipid nanoparticle|lipid nanoparticle]] so the fragile strand survives the blood and gets into cells. mRNA's great strengths are speed and flexibility — a new drug needs only a new sequence, made by the same [[In vitro transcription|in vitro transcription]] process — but its effect is transient (the protein fades as the strand decays) and delivery beyond the liver and injection site remains hard.
