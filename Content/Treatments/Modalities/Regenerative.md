A **regenerative** therapy replaces cells or tissue the body has lost — the insulin-making cells destroyed in type 1 diabetes, the dopamine neurons lost in Parkinson's, worn-out cartilage — with new, healthy cells grown outside the body and transplanted in. Unlike a [[Cell therapy|cell therapy]], which re-arms immune cells to attack, the goal here is to supply working _replacement_ cells of a specific type.

The usual starting material is a pluripotent stem cell, most often an [[Induced pluripotent stem cell|induced pluripotent stem cell]] — an adult cell reprogrammed so it can become any cell type — which is then coaxed, through a long timed sequence of chemical signals, down the developmental path to the wanted mature cell. The pipeline is built around two hard problems carried through every step: driving that differentiation fully and reproducibly to a mature, functional cell, and the overriding safety task of removing every leftover stem cell, since any that remain can keep dividing and form a tumour. As a living, engrafting therapy it carries long-term follow-up and a specialized regulatory route.
