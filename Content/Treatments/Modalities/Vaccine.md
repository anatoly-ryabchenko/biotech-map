A **vaccine** does not treat a disease directly — it teaches the immune system to recognize a threat in advance, so that if the real pathogen (or a tumour) later appears, the body destroys it before it takes hold. The thing the immune system is taught to recognize is an [[Antigen|antigen]]: a piece of the threat, usually one of its surface proteins.

The pipeline pins down which antigen to present and in what shape (often the hardest part — many viral proteins must be locked in a specific conformation to provoke protective antibodies), and pairs it with an [[Adjuvant|adjuvant]] that amplifies the immune response. The antigen can be delivered in several forms — a purified protein, an mRNA instructing the body to make it, a harmless carrier virus, or a killed pathogen. Because vaccines are given to vast numbers of _healthy_ people, the safety bar is exceptionally high, and proving they work means waiting for natural exposure in very large trials. Vaccine development runs its own combined clinical track rather than the separate phases other modalities use.
