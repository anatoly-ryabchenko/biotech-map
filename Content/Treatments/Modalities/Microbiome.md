A **microbiome** medicine — a _live biotherapeutic product_ — treats disease with living bacteria. The human gut holds trillions of microbes that perform functions our own cells cannot, and when that community is disrupted, disease can follow; a microbiome drug introduces selected strains to restore a missing function or crowd out a harmful organism. The drug is not a molecule but one or more living, self-replicating strains.

The pipeline finds responsible microbes by comparing the gut communities of healthy people and patients (using [[Metagenomics|metagenomics]]), isolates and characterizes promising strains, and confirms they can take up residence and do their job. A central design choice, carried through the pipeline, is a single strain versus a deliberately assembled _defined consortium_ of several — a controlled, manufacturable alternative to transplanting whole donor stool. The defining challenges are that many gut bacteria are hard to grow outside the body (especially the oxygen-sensitive ones), keeping the living product viable through manufacturing, and getting strains to engraft against an established gut community.
