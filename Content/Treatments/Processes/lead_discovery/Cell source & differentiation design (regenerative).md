Regenerative medicine aims to replace cells or tissue the body has lost — the insulin-making cells destroyed in type 1 diabetes, the neurons lost in Parkinson's, worn-out cartilage — with new, healthy cells grown outside the body. Unlike a cell therapy, which re-arms immune cells to attack, the goal here is to supply working replacement cells of a particular type. This step makes two linked design choices: where the cells come from, and what they must become.

The usual starting material is a _pluripotent stem cell_ — a cell that can divide indefinitely and turn into any cell type in the body. The leading source is the [[Induced pluripotent stem cell|induced pluripotent stem cell]] (iPSC): an ordinary adult cell, taken from skin, blood, or even urine, reprogrammed back to that unspecialized state. This forces the central source decision — _autologous_ versus _allogeneic_. Autologous cells come from the patient, so they are immune-matched and won't be rejected, but every batch is a bespoke product built from scratch for one person. Allogeneic cells come from a single carefully chosen "universal" donor and are banked to treat many patients off the shelf — far cheaper and faster — but the recipient's immune system may attack them unless they are further engineered to hide.

The second choice is the _differentiation route_: the planned sequence of chemical signals that will coax the stem cells, step by step, down the developmental path to the wanted mature cell type, mimicking the cues the body uses as an embryo develops. Designing the route means knowing which signaling molecules to add, in what order, and for how long, to march the cells through the correct intermediate stages.

The characteristic difficulty is that differentiation is never complete or perfectly pure: a batch ends up a mixture, and any leftover undifferentiated stem cells are dangerous, because they keep their power to divide without limit and can form tumors. So the route must drive cells fully to the target type, and a way to detect and remove stragglers has to be built in — problems carried into the next step. The output is a chosen cell source and a draft differentiation route.
