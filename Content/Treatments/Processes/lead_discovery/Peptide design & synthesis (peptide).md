A peptide is a short chain of amino acids — the same building blocks proteins are made of, but only a handful to a few dozen long. That size is deliberate: large enough to grip a broad, flat target surface (the kind of protein-to-protein contact small molecules struggle to disrupt), yet small enough to be built by chemistry rather than grown inside living cells. Many of the body's own signaling molecules are peptides — insulin, the gut hormone GLP-1 — which gives this modality a rich supply of natural starting points.

There are two ways to arrive at a starting peptide. One is to begin from a natural peptide that already acts on the target — a hormone, or a fragment of the protein the target normally binds — and propose changes to it. The other is to search a vast library for binders using a _display_ method: [[Phage display|phage display]], and the related mRNA- and ribosome-display techniques, physically tie each peptide to the genetic code that specifies it, so billions of random peptides can be washed over the target at once and the few that stick identified by reading their attached code.

However it is found, the peptide is then made by [[Solid-phase peptide synthesis|solid-phase peptide synthesis]]: the chain is assembled one amino acid at a time on a tiny solid resin bead — couple the next residue, strip off its protective cap, couple the next — and finally cleaved off the bead. This is a wholly different process from a small molecule's bespoke chemical route, which is why peptides are their own modality even where the steps on either side look similar.

The characteristic difficulty is that natural peptides make poor drugs. A floppy, open chain is chopped to pieces by the body's protein-cutting enzymes within minutes and filtered out by the kidneys almost as fast, and a free chain also pays a stiff energetic penalty to fold into its binding shape each time. Design therefore builds in defenses from the start, introduced here as variants and pushed hard in optimization: closing the chain into a ring (cyclization), adding a chemical "staple" that locks its folded shape, or using mirror-image (D-) amino acids that the body's enzymes fail to recognize.

The output is one or more peptides that genuinely bind the target, ready to be optimized for potency and staying power.
