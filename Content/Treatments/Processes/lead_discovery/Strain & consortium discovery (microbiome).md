The human gut holds trillions of bacteria that carry out functions our own cells cannot, and when that community is thrown out of balance, disease can follow. A microbiome medicine — a _live biotherapeutic product_ — treats disease by introducing living bacteria to restore a missing function or crowd out a harmful organism. The drug is not a molecule but one or more living strains. This step finds them.

The search starts from biology: comparing the gut communities of healthy people with those of patients to spot which microbes, or which functions, are missing in disease. The central tool is [[Metagenomics|metagenomics]] — sequencing all the DNA in a stool sample at once to identify which microbes are present and what they are collectively capable of, without having to grow any of them first. Organisms that look responsible are then physically _isolated_: grown out from healthy-donor samples into pure cultures, one strain at a time, and screened for the wanted activity — producing a particular beneficial molecule, blocking a pathogen, calming inflammation.

A central design choice, narrated as the modality's two variants, is single strain versus _defined consortium_. A single, well-characterized strain is the simplest possible drug. But many gut functions are shared across several species, so a deliberately assembled cocktail of a handful of strains — a defined consortium — can be more robust and do more, while still being fully known. A defined consortium is the controlled, manufacturable alternative to transplanting whole stool from a donor, whose exact contents can be neither fully characterized nor kept consistent from batch to batch.

The characteristic difficulty is that many gut bacteria are hard to grow outside the body: they are killed by oxygen and depend on nutrients and partner species found only in the gut, so a strain that looks ideal in the sequencing data may be impossible to culture — and an organism's behaviour in a clean dish often differs from its behaviour inside a living, competitive gut community. The output is a set of candidate strains, or a draft consortium, ready to be characterized and selected.
