This approach designs a brand-new protein from scratch completely [[In silico|in silico]], shaped specifically to act on the target as well as not to react with non-targets ([[Negative design|negative design]]). Building and testing real proteins is slow and expensive, so the idea is to get the design as right as possible on the screen first.
What it means to "act on the target" is not one thing, and the choice ripples through every step. A _blocker_ just binds a surface the target needs and shuts it down by occupancy; a _conformational modulator_ binds so as to push the target into a particular shape, switching it on or off, or otherwise changing what it does; an _enzyme_ goes further and chemically transforms a [[Substrate|substrate]]. These get progressively harder to design — and, crucially, harder to verify in silico.

The work runs in three principal steps, however there may exist models that fuse some or all of those steps.

**First comes shape.** Designers discover what three-dimensional form the new protein should take for proper [[Docking|docking]] (interacting / binding to the target). At this stage, the desired interaction with the target is already known from upstream processes. The discovery of the desired shape is done through [[Backbone-generation networks|backbone-generation networks]] - they accept a target, the hotspot residues (part of the target it should interact with), and propose structures that are predicted to interact desirably with the target. Important goal in this step is to produce a shape that is actually "designable" — for which some real sequence will reliably fold up and hold steady. Since this stage can have only limited modeling of designability, later stages may result in no sequence that is predicted to fold into the desired shape and a new iteration might be needed.
What counts as the desired shape therefore depends on the mode: an interface complementing the target's surface for a blocker; an interface that fits one specific conformational state for a modulator; or a pocket that cradles a substrate and holds catalytic residues in precise geometry for an enzyme. The latter two often need the backbone generated _around_ a small molecule, [[Cofactor|cofactor]], or substrate rather than around protein hotspots alone — which is what all-atom backbone-generation networks are for.

**Second comes sequence.** A protein is a chain of amino-acid building blocks that folds into a 3D shape which dictates its properties and how it reacts to other molecules. The folding of a protein follows [[Protein folding principles|protein folding principles]]. Once a desired shape is chosen, the designer must find a chain of amino acids that will actually _be foldable_ in that exact shape. This is the reverse of normal folding — going from shape back to sequence — and is performed by [[Inverse-folding networks|inverse-folding networks]], which accept a desired structure and propose amino-acid chains that are predicted to _be foldable_ into this structure. Note that "foldable into" does not mean it _will_ fold there. It means the target shape is a stable, low-energy arrangement for that sequence — if you could place the chain in that shape, it would hold rather than unravel. But the sequence may have an even more stable fold it would settle into instead, which is why we need the third step.

**Third comes checking**, which is really two questions. 
_Does it fold?_ — This is done via [[Structure-prediction networks|structure-prediction networks]], which take the proposed sequence and forecast the shape it would really adopt. If the predicted shape matches the intended one closely, the design is considered promising; if not, it is discarded or revised before anyone touches a lab bench. Since the goal of designed proteins is to act on a target and do not act on negative targets, they are also folded together with the target and negative targets to confirm they dock (or not in case of negative targets) as planned.
_Does it do the intended thing?_
For a modulator, we need to check not only that the protein folds with the target, but that binding actually pushes the target into the desired conformation. For an enzyme, the task is harder still: it isn't enough for the protein to bind: its active site must hold the catalytic groups in the precise geometry that drives a chemical reaction, ideally cycling through the right sequence of states to convert substrate into product. This can be probed with [[Molecular dynamics|molecular dynamics]] (MD), a physics simulation that, instead of predicting one static shape, computes the forces between every atom and steps them forward in tiny increments to produce a trajectory — a movie of how the system moves rather than a single frame. In case of enzymes, it also involves direct physical models ([[Quantum Mechanics - Molecular Mechanics]] - QM/MM) or [[Neural network approaches for chemical interactions|neural network approaches for chemical interactions]], since traditional MD does not model chemical bond formation / breaking. Both modulator and enzyme design are active research areas rather than routine pipelines, with enzymes the harder of the two — designs typically emerge weak and still need lab-based directed evolution (part of processes that follow) to become genuinely useful.

The prediction tools used above are learned models trained largely on isolated protein structures (mostly static), so they have limited (if any) consideration of the actual environment the protein sits in while it is [[Protein expression|expressed]] or folded — temperature, pH, crowding, binding partners, the expression host, and so on. As a result, many computationally designed proteins earn highly confident prediction scores yet still fail to express, fold, or stay stable under real experimental conditions. This is especially true with conformation modulators and enzymes, since they require additional, less precise steps. Worse, because the inverse-folding networks and structure-prediction networks often share training data and assumptions, a gap in that data — a missing kind of structure, or a lack of negative examples — produces the same mistake in both networks at once, so the design passes its check and becomes a false positive. Because these models are trained largely on natural proteins, they are implicitly biased toward what evolution has already explored — and a genuinely novel design sits in exactly the region where that training gives the least guidance.
For this reason, this is a highly iterative process with wet lab validation step.

Typically, this process produces thousands of potential structures, with a few candidate chains selected for each. Because the wet-lab step is slow and expensive, these candidates first have their desirable properties predicted in silico (folding confidence, interface/binding quality, and so on - see [[Protein properties prediction|protein properties prediction]]), and are then filtered down to dozens to hundreds of molecules and ranked. 

These final candidates are sent to the next stage — wet-lab validation.
