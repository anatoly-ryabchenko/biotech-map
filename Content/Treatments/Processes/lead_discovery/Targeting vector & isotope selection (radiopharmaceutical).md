A radiopharmaceutical destroys diseased cells by delivering radiation to them from the inside. It has three parts bolted together: a _targeting vector_ that homes to the diseased cell, a radioactive atom (a _radioisotope_) that does the killing, and a [[Chelator|chelator]] — a cage-like molecule that grips the radioactive metal and ties it to the vector. This step chooses all three.

The targeting vector is a molecule that binds a marker found on the diseased cells: for prostate cancer, small molecules that latch onto the surface protein PSMA; for other tumors, peptides or antibodies raised against their markers. The isotope is chosen by the kind of radiation it gives off. _Beta emitters_ such as lutetium-177 throw out particles that travel about a millimetre, irradiating a small neighbourhood of cells — useful for a sizeable tumor mass, and forgiving of cells the vector missed. _Alpha emitters_ such as actinium-225 release much heavier particles that travel only a few cell-widths but do far more damage along the way, sterilizing single cells and microscopic deposits while sparing tissue just beyond.

A defining feature of this modality is the [[Theranostics|theranostic]] pair: the same vector and chelator can carry either a gentle imaging isotope, which reveals on a scan exactly where the drug goes in a given patient, or a therapeutic isotope, which treats. So the scan first confirms the target is actually present and picks out who will benefit, and the matched therapy is then given to those patients only. The chelator must hold whichever metal it carries extremely tightly, because a radioactive atom that escapes its cage drifts off and irradiates the wrong organ.

The characteristic difficulty is that everything turns on the radiation reaching only the intended place: a vector that lingers in the blood or sticks to healthy organs dumps its dose there, and the bone marrow and kidneys are especially vulnerable. Choosing a vector that binds quickly and clears cleanly, and a chelator that never lets go, is the heart of the design. The output is a chosen vector–chelator–isotope combination, ready for the radiochemistry to be worked out.
