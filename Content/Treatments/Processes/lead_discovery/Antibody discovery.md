An antibody is a Y-shaped protein the immune system makes to recognize one specific target with great precision: the tips of the Y form a binding surface shaped to grip a particular spot, an [[Epitope|epitope]], on the target. That natural specificity is what makes antibodies such valuable drugs — they can latch onto a chosen protein and almost nothing else. This step finds a [[Monoclonal antibody|monoclonal antibody]]: a single, exactly-defined antibody, made identically every time, that binds the target. For an antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) the very same discovery work produces the antibody that will later be loaded with a toxic payload — here it is found simply as a binder.

Four routes are used, and most programs run more than one in parallel.

**Immunization and hybridoma.** Inject the target into a mouse, let its immune system raise a response against it, then harvest the antibody-producing B cells from the spleen and fuse each with an immortal tumor cell so it divides forever — a _hybridoma_, a permanent factory for one antibody. This is the historical workhorse. Together with the transgenic-mouse route below, which also uses hybridomas, immunization lies behind most approved antibodies — though antibodies from ordinary mice are mouse-derived and need humanizing later.

**Transgenic mice.** The same approach but in mice engineered to carry human antibody genes, so the antibodies emerge essentially human from the start.

**Phage display.** A fully laboratory route with no animal: vast libraries of antibody fragments are displayed on the surface of [[Phage display|bacteriophages]] (viruses that infect bacteria) and washed over the target through several rounds, each round enriching the ones that stick.

**Single B cell screening.** Antigen-specific B cells are picked out one at a time — often from an immunized animal or a recovered patient — and their antibody genes read directly.

The output is a _panel_ of antibodies that bind the target. The characteristic difficulty is that a first binder is rarely good on every axis at once — binding strength, the exact epitope it hits, and (for animal-derived antibodies) being recognized as foreign by the human immune system — so a panel is carried forward to be refined rather than a single winner crowned.
