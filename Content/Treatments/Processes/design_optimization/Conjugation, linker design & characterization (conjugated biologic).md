An antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) is a targeting antibody chemically bolted to a potent cell-killing drug, so the antibody carries the poison straight to the diseased cell and spares the rest of the body. The antibody has been discovered, matured, and made developable; this step attaches the payload and confirms exactly what was made — a chemistry problem distinct enough to stand as its own step.

Three things are chosen and joined. The _payload_ is a toxin far too poisonous to give on its own — usually one that wrecks a dividing cell's internal scaffolding or shreds its DNA. The _linker_ is the chemical tether between antibody and payload, and its design is decisive. A _cleavable_ linker is built to come apart only under conditions found inside the target cell — its acidity, or specific cutting enzymes — releasing the drug where it is wanted; a _non-cleavable_ linker holds on until the whole antibody is digested inside the cell. Either way the linker must be rock-stable in the bloodstream, because a payload that drops off in circulation poisons healthy tissue, the main source of ADC side effects.

The third variable is the _drug-to-antibody ratio_ (DAR): how many payload molecules, on average, ride each antibody. Too few and the ADC is weak; too many and the antibody clumps, is cleared faster, and turns toxic, because most payloads are _hydrophobic_ (water-repelling) and make the whole molecule sticky. Older ADCs settled at about three to four; newer ones such as trastuzumab deruxtecan (Enhertu) carry about eight, made possible by water-friendly linkers that offset the payload's stickiness and by payloads less poisonous per molecule. Older chemistry attaches payloads at random surface points, giving a messy mixture of different DARs; modern _site-specific_ conjugation fixes them at defined positions, yielding a uniform, better-behaved product.

_Characterization_ then measures what actually resulted: the average DAR and its spread, where the payloads landed, how much unattached payload remains, and whether the antibody still binds and hasn't aggregated. The characteristic difficulty is heterogeneity — conjugation is a statistical chemical reaction, so a batch is really a distribution of molecules, and an uncontrolled spread of DAR means inconsistent potency and safety. When the chemistry won't behave, the work can loop back toward the antibody. The output is a defined, well-characterized conjugate, ready for formulation.
