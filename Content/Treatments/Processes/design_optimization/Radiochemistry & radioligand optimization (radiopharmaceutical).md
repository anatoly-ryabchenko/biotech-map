The vector, chelator, and isotope were chosen; this step works out the actual chemistry of joining them and tunes the assembled molecule so its radiation lands on the target and nowhere else. The result is a _radioligand_: the targeting molecule, its [[Chelator|chelator]] cage, and the radioactive metal locked inside.

Several things are optimized together. The _radiolabeling_ reaction — loading the radioactive metal into the chelator — must be fast, near-complete, and gentle, because the isotope is decaying the whole time: a slow or inefficient reaction wastes precious activity and leaves cold, unlabelled molecules competing with the active ones for the target. A high _specific activity_ — a large share of molecules actually carrying a radioactive atom — is therefore a goal. The molecule's _pharmacokinetics_ are tuned by adjusting the linkers and spacers between vector and chelator: the ideal radioligand reaches and sticks to its target quickly, then clears from blood and healthy tissue rapidly, so the radiation dwells on the tumor and not on the body at large. _Stability_ is checked hard — the chelator must not let go of its metal in the body, and the molecule must resist being torn apart by the very radiation it carries (radiolysis), which can degrade a whole batch from the inside.

The characteristic difficulty is that everything happens against a ticking clock and a self-destructive cargo: an alpha or beta emitter is damaging its own carrier and decaying away even as the drug is assembled, shipped, and injected, so the chemistry must be quick and the product used fast — and any molecule that clears too slowly, or a chelator that leaks, delivers its dose to the bone marrow and kidneys instead. When the assembled radioligand cannot be made to behave, the work loops back to reconsider the vector, chelator, or isotope. The output is an optimized radioligand, ready for preclinical targeting and dosimetry studies.
