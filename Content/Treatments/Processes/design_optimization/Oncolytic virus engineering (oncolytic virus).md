The design step decided the backbone and the planned genetic changes; this step actually builds the engineered virus and tunes it until it is selective, potent, and safe. The construction is real molecular work: the chosen deletions — the viral genes that confine the virus to tumor cells — and the insertions — the immune-recruiting gene such as GM-CSF — are made in the virus's genome, often by first assembling the entire large viral genome inside bacteria and then reviving live virus from it.

The engineered virus is then put through cell tests and refined on two axes that pull against each other. _Selectivity_ is verified and tightened: the virus must replicate vigorously in cancer cells of the intended type while barely growing in normal ones, and if it harms healthy cells further weakening changes are made. _Potency_ is pushed the other way: the virus must still infect, spread between, and burst tumor cells fast enough to matter, and rouse a strong immune response as it does. Extra genes are sometimes added to help — for example ones that break down the dense tissue scaffolding around a tumor so the virus can spread through it.

The characteristic difficulty is the same tension named at design, now made concrete: each safety-driven weakening of the virus also blunts its spread and killing, while restoring potency risks toxicity or lets the virus replicate where it shouldn't. On top of this, the body's antiviral immunity attacks the virus itself, often clearing it before it finishes its work — so the engineering must keep the virus effective despite being a target. When no balance can be struck, the work loops back to reconsider the backbone or the set of changes. The output is a fully built, characterized oncolytic virus, ready to be formulated and delivered into tumors.
