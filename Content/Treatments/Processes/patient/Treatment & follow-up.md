This is the shared terminal the whole pipeline has been building toward, and it covers everything that happens once a medicine is approved: getting the right therapy into the right patient safely, and then watching it over years in the real world.

**Delivering the treatment.** How the therapy is given depends entirely on what it is: a pill swallowed at home; an injection under the skin or a slow infusion in a clinic; a gene therapy delivered into the blood or a specific organ; a virus injected into a tumour; a radioactive drug after which the patient briefly emits radiation; a simple vaccine shot; a swallowed capsule of live bacteria. The care setting matches the risk — a pill needs little supervision, while a cell-therapy infusion or a strong antibody can trigger dangerous reactions and so is given under close hospital monitoring — after CAR-T cells, for instance, patients stay within reach of the treatment centre and do not drive for about two weeks, a period the FDA halved in 2025 once experience showed these reactions could be managed without its earlier special restrictions. For many targeted medicines a [[Companion diagnostic|companion diagnostic]] is run first — a test confirming the patient's disease actually carries the target the drug acts on — so the right drug reaches the right person; for a radiopharmaceutical, the matched imaging scan plays that role.

**Watching it in the real world.** A trial of a few thousand cannot reveal everything. Once a drug is used by far larger and more varied populations — older, sicker, on other medicines — rare harms and long-term effects can finally surface. This ongoing monitoring is [[Pharmacovigilance|pharmacovigilance]]: gathering and analyzing reports of adverse events to detect safety signals a trial was too small or too short to see. Alongside it runs the essential, unglamorous work of keeping the drug reliably manufactured and supplied for years.

**Long-term follow-up for the advanced therapies.** Gene therapies, gene-edited products, engineered cells, regenerative cells, and oncolytic viruses make permanent or long-lasting changes to the body, so their dangers can appear long after treatment — and watching for them is mandated for years. The chief worry is a delayed cancer arising from a gene landing in the wrong place ([[Insertional mutagenesis|insertional mutagenesis]]), but also new blood or autoimmune disorders and the lingering effects of long-lived cells. Regulators set the duration by risk: up to about five years for vectors that stay separate from the genome, and as long as fifteen years for ones that integrate into or edit the patient's DNA. The same follow-up also shows how _durable_ the benefit is — whether a one-time therapy keeps working for years. The characteristic difficulty is that the worst risks are rare and slow, so they cannot be ruled out before approval, only watched for, and keeping patients enrolled and traceable over a decade is itself hard.

This is where treatment is delivered and then watched; there is no later stage to advance to. When real-world surveillance uncovers a serious new risk, it loops back to confirmatory testing — to a large clinical study, or for a vaccine to its own clinical track — to settle the question rigorously rather than moving forward on incomplete evidence.
