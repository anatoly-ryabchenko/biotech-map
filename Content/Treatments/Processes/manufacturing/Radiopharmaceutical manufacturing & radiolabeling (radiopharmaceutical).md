Manufacturing a radiopharmaceutical is dominated by one fact: the radioactive isotope is decaying from the moment it is made, so the finished drug has a short usable life — sometimes hours, at most days — and cannot be stockpiled. This collapses formulation and manufacturing into a single race against the clock, run under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]] but with logistics no other modality faces.

Two pieces are made. The _cold_ component — the targeting vector with its [[Chelator|chelator]] attached — is a stable molecule that can be made and stored in advance, like an ordinary drug. The _radioisotope_ (lutetium-177, with a 6.7-day half-life, or actinium-225) is produced separately in reactors or accelerators. The decisive act is _radiolabeling_: loading the radioactive metal into the chelator, done late and close to the patient. Because of the short half-life, this is increasingly _decentralized_ — the cold, chelator-bearing molecule is shipped as a "kit" and the isotope added at a regional radiopharmacy, or even the hospital, shortly before use, with production schedules corrected for ongoing decay and everything moved under specialized radioactive-materials handling.

The characteristic difficulty is time and radioactivity together: every hour of delay loses dose to decay, so manufacture, quality testing, shipping, and dosing must all fit inside a tight window; the work must shield staff from radiation; and the radiation can degrade the product from within during the short time it exists. There is little margin to repeat a failed batch. The output is a freshly radiolabeled, quality-checked dose, dispatched to reach the patient before it decays away.
