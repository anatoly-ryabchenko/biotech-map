A peptide is built by chemistry, not grown in cells, so its manufacturing is the industrial-scale version of the [[Solid-phase peptide synthesis|solid-phase peptide synthesis]] used in discovery: the chain is assembled one amino acid at a time on a solid resin — couple the next residue, strip its protective cap, couple the next — then cleaved off. Scaling this to the kilograms a successful peptide drug needs is its own discipline, run under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]].

The defining challenge is purity, and it is baked into the chemistry. Each coupling step is efficient but not perfect, so a tiny fraction of chains fail to add a residue at every cycle, and over the dozens of cycles needed for a long peptide these failures accumulate into a swarm of closely related wrong sequences — chains missing or carrying an extra residue — that are chemically very similar to the correct one and hard to separate. The product is therefore heavily purified, usually by _reverse-phase high-performance liquid chromatography_ (pushing the mixture through a column that separates molecules by subtle differences in how strongly they stick), sometimes more than once. Peptides that must form internal bridges — a disulphide bond, a cyclization — have those reactions done and controlled at scale too.

The characteristic difficulty is this build-up of deletion impurities together with enormous solvent use: every synthesis cycle and every purification consumes large volumes of solvent, making peptide manufacturing wasteful and costly — which is why longer peptides are expensive and why the field is pushing toward greener and partly enzymatic routes. The output is a purified, correctly folded peptide drug substance, made to specification and ready for trials.
