A live biotherapeutic is manufactured by _growing the bacteria_ — fermenting the selected strain or consortium to large numbers, then concentrating, preserving, and packaging them as a living product, under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]]. Because the drug is living organisms, the process must keep them alive and viable, and the "dose" is a count of live bacteria.

The work runs in steps. Each strain is grown by fermentation — and because many beneficial gut bacteria are killed by oxygen, this often has to be done _anaerobically_, in sealed, oxygen-free vessels, which is harder than ordinary fermentation. The bacteria are then harvested, concentrated, and usually _freeze-dried_ with protective ingredients into a stable powder that survives storage, then filled — commonly into capsules designed to pass through stomach acid intact and release the bacteria in the gut. For a defined consortium, the several strains are typically grown separately and then blended to a fixed composition.

The characteristic difficulty is keeping fragile living organisms alive and consistent through a harsh process: anaerobic strains die on exposure to air, freeze-drying stresses and kills a fraction of the cells (so the process must be tuned to maximize survival), and a multi-strain consortium must come out at the right ratio every time even though its strains grow at different rates. Confirming the product is the right organisms, viable, and free of contaminating microbes is the core quality task. The output is a viable, defined, packaged live-bacterial product, ready for trials.
