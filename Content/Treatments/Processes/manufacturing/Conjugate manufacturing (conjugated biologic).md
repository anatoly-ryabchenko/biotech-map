Making an antibody–drug conjugate at scale is really three manufacturing problems chained together, all under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]]. First the antibody is produced by the same living-cell biomanufacturing used for any protein biologic — grown in CHO cells and purified. Separately, the toxic payload and its linker are made by chemical synthesis, like a small-molecule drug; because the payload is extremely poisonous, this is done in specially contained facilities to protect workers. Then the two are joined in the _conjugation_ reaction, and the finished conjugate purified.

The conjugation step is the crux and the hardest to control. The payload-linker is chemically attached to the antibody, and the reaction must hit a tightly defined _drug-to-antibody ratio_ — the average number of payloads per antibody — with as little spread as possible, since too few weakens the drug and too many makes it unstable and toxic. Older random-attachment chemistry yields a mixture that must be characterized exhaustively; modern site-specific methods give a more uniform product. After conjugation, unreacted free payload and mis-loaded species are purified away.

The characteristic difficulty is doing reproducible chemistry on a giant, fragile biological molecule: the conjugation must modify the antibody precisely without unfolding or clumping it, every batch must land on the same drug-to-antibody ratio and distribution, and the highly potent payload demands rigorous containment and the removal of every trace of free toxin. The output is a purified, well-characterized conjugate drug substance with a controlled drug-to-antibody ratio, ready for trials.
