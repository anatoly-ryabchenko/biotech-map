A regenerative product is manufactured by running the differentiation protocol at scale: starting from a bank of [[Induced pluripotent stem cell|pluripotent stem cells]], expanding them, then driving them step by step down the developmental path to the wanted mature cell type — all under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]]. Unlike an autologous cell therapy, many regenerative products are _allogeneic_, made from a single well-characterized donor stem-cell line and banked, so one production run can yield many doses — making this closer to a scalable batch process.

The work is to take a hand-optimized lab protocol and make it big, consistent, and pure. The stem cells are grown to large numbers, then taken through the timed sequence of signaling molecules that coaxes them into, say, insulin-making cells or neurons, in controlled vessels rather than dishes. The batch is then tested for identity, purity, function, and — the non-negotiable release test — the absence of leftover undifferentiated stem cells.

The characteristic difficulty is that differentiation is a long, multi-step biological program that is hard to keep identical from batch to batch: small variations compound across the many steps, so the yield and the proportion of fully matured cells drift, and any residual pluripotent cell that slips through can form a tumour in the patient. Achieving full maturity at scale, and proving the batch is free of dangerous stragglers, are the central manufacturing hurdles. The output is a released batch of differentiated, mature, stem-cell-free cells, ready for trials.
