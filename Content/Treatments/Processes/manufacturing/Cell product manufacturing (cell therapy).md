For most cell therapies the "manufacturing" is the personalized engineering-and-growing run described earlier — collect the patient's T cells, activate them, insert the receptor gene with the vector, and expand them — now done under full [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]] as the actual drug-making step, followed by the release testing that decides whether the batch can be given to the patient. The [[Lentiviral vector|lentiviral vector]] that delivers the receptor gene is itself produced to GMP standard as an input to this step (the same vector-growing problem gene therapies face), rather than as a separate finished product.

What makes this unlike all other manufacturing is that it cannot be centralized or pooled. Each _autologous_ batch is made from one patient's cells and can treat only that patient, so the industry must run thousands of tiny, parallel, individually-tracked manufacturing runs instead of a few large ones — a model called _scale-out_ rather than scale-up. The whole loop, from drawing the patient's cells to returning the finished product — the _vein-to-vein time_ — typically takes two to four weeks, during which a very sick patient waits.

Before release, the product is tested: cell count and viability, how efficiently the receptor gene was inserted, sterility, and the absence of contaminating microbes or any accidentally replication-competent virus. _Sterility testing_ is usually the slowest of these, and the bottleneck on getting the product to the patient.

The characteristic difficulty is the collision of individuality, time pressure, and quality: the starting cells differ from patient to patient and a run can simply fail; there is no backup batch; the patient cannot wait long; and full release testing is slow — so the field is pushing hard toward automation, "GMP-in-a-box" closed systems, and even bedside manufacturing. The output is a released, single-patient cell-therapy product, ready to be administered in trials.
