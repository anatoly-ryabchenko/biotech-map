An oncolytic virus is a live, replicating virus, so manufacturing means growing it: infecting cultured cells with the engineered virus, letting them produce many copies, then harvesting and purifying it — all under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]]. It resembles both vaccine-virus production and viral-vector manufacturing — the virus is grown in a permissive producer cell line in bioreactors, the cells are broken open to release it, and it is purified away from cell debris.

A defining requirement is keeping the virus alive and infectious throughout. Unlike a vector engineered to be replication-_defective_, an oncolytic virus must stay able to replicate — that is its mechanism — so the process has to preserve potency (measured as the number of infectious virus particles) while still removing contaminants and host-cell material. The product's identity and genetic stability are checked to confirm the engineered deletions and inserted genes are intact and that the virus has not mutated during growth.

The characteristic difficulty is this balance between purity and potency for a fragile live agent: harsh purification that removes contaminants can also inactivate virus, virus yields swing with the health of the producer cells, and the virus must be confirmed genetically stable — that it has not, over the many rounds of replication needed to make a batch, drifted back toward a more dangerous or less tumour-selective form. The output is a purified, potent, genetically verified live-virus drug substance, ready for trials.
