How a vaccine is manufactured depends entirely on its platform: a protein-subunit vaccine is grown in cells like any protein biologic; an mRNA vaccine is made by in-vitro transcription and wrapped in lipid nanoparticles; a viral-vector vaccine is grown in cultured cells; a whole inactivated vaccine is grown and then killed. Whatever the route, the work runs under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]], and the antigen is combined with its [[Adjuvant|adjuvant]] and filled into vials or syringes.

What sets vaccine manufacturing apart is the weight placed on _lot release_ — the testing of every single batch ("lot") before it can be used. Because vaccines are given to enormous numbers of _healthy_ people, often children, and because they work by provoking the immune system, consistency between batches is paramount and enforced unusually strictly: each lot is tested for identity, purity, sterility, potency (that it raises the expected immune response, often measured against a reference), and safety, and for many vaccines a national regulatory laboratory independently reviews and must release each lot before it ships.

The characteristic difficulty is making a biological product identical batch after batch, at the vast scale and low cost mass vaccination requires — billions of doses for a global campaign — while meeting that exceptionally strict consistency and safety bar, and often needing a cold chain to keep the product stable. A single failed or contaminated lot reaching healthy recipients is intolerable, which is why batch testing is so heavy. The output is released, lot-tested vaccine, ready for clinical development and, after approval, mass supply.
