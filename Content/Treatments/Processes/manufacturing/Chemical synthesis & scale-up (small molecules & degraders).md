Both small molecules and degraders are built by chemical synthesis, and making a few grams of a compound in a research lab is a different problem from making hundreds of kilograms reliably and purely, batch after batch — this step solves the second. The route the medicinal chemists used to discover the molecule is usually unfit for scale: too many steps, dangerous reagents, low yields, costly purifications. So _process chemists_ design a new route — fewer steps, safer and cheaper reagents, reactions that behave predictably in large vessels, where heat and mixing work very differently from a flask. (A degrader adds the extra steps of joining its two binding ends through their linker, but the discipline is the same.)

The work runs under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|Good Manufacturing Practice]] (GMP), the binding quality system governing all medicine-making: every material tested, every step documented, every batch traceable and reproducible. A central preoccupation is _impurity control_ — byproducts of the synthesis must be identified and driven below strict limits set by international (ICH) guidelines, with special vigilance for any that could damage DNA. Impurities are easier to purge early, so the route is arranged so its final steps — typically a _crystallization_ that grows the pure drug as a solid while leaving impurities behind in solution — deliver the clean, correct solid form chosen back in formulation.

The characteristic difficulty is that chemistry does not simply scale: a reaction gentle in a flask can overheat or run away in a large reactor, mixing turns uneven, and a crystallization that gave the right crystal at small scale can give a different, unwanted form (or trap solvent) in a big one — any of which changes the drug's purity or behaviour. The output is a validated, scalable process making the drug substance to specification, ready to supply trials and, ultimately, the market.
