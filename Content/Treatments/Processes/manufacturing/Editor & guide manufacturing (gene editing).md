A gene-editing medicine is not one molecule but a set of components that must each be manufactured and then combined: the [[CRISPR|CRISPR]] editing protein, the guide RNA that aims it, and — for in-body editing — the vehicle that carries them in. Each is made by a different process, all under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]].

The editing protein (Cas9, or a base- or prime-editor) is most often supplied not as protein but as the _mRNA_ that encodes it, made by the same in-vitro-transcription process as an mRNA drug, so the patient's cells briefly make the editor and then stop — a deliberate choice, since a short-lived editor makes fewer stray edits. The guide RNA is made by chemical synthesis like an oligonucleotide, with stabilizing modifications. For in-body editing these are then packaged together into [[Lipid nanoparticle|lipid nanoparticles]]; for editing done on cells outside the body, the components may instead be delivered as a pre-formed protein-plus-guide complex.

The characteristic difficulty is that several distinct manufacturing streams — an mRNA, a synthetic guide, a delivery particle — must each meet pharmaceutical quality and then be combined into one consistent product, multiplying the ways a batch can go wrong; and the editor's activity must be high enough to edit efficiently yet controlled enough not to linger and cut elsewhere. The output is a complete, quality-controlled set of editing components (commonly mRNA plus guide in a lipid nanoparticle), ready for trials.
