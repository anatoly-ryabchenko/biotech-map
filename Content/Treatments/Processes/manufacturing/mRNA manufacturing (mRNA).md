mRNA is neither grown in cells nor assembled letter-by-letter; it is made in a single enzymatic reaction, [[In vitro transcription|in vitro transcription]], in which an enzyme reads a DNA template and spins out many copies of the mRNA strand. This makes mRNA fast and flexible to manufacture — a new sequence needs only a new template — which is why mRNA vaccines could be produced at unprecedented speed. The process runs under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]].

It runs in steps. First a DNA _template_ is grown in bacteria as a circular plasmid and cut open into a straight line. The transcription reaction copies it into mRNA, building in the [[Modified nucleosides|modified nucleosides]], the cap (increasingly added during the reaction itself), and the poly-A tail. Then comes the hardest part, _purification_: the product must be separated from the DNA template, the enzymes, leftover building blocks, and — above all — _double-stranded RNA_, the accidental byproduct that trips the body's antiviral alarms; chromatography steps are tuned specifically to strip it out, a major quality and regulatory focus. Finally the purified mRNA is wrapped into [[Lipid nanoparticle|lipid nanoparticles]] by rapid microfluidic mixing.

The characteristic difficulty is that the great strength of mRNA manufacturing — a clean, cell-free reaction — is undercut by the difficulty of purification and packaging at scale: removing double-stranded RNA without sacrificing yield is hard, the lipid-nanoparticle step is finicky and expensive, and the raw materials (capping reagents, modified nucleotides, specialized lipids) are costly and supply-limited. The output is purified mRNA encapsulated in lipid nanoparticles, made to specification and ready for trials.
