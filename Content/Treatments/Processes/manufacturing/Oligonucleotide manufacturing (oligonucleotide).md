Like a peptide, an oligonucleotide is built by chemistry on a solid support — but using _phosphoramidite_ chemistry, the decades-old gold standard, which adds one genetic letter at a time in a four-step cycle: couple the next building block, cap any chains that failed to react, stabilize the new link, then uncap for the next addition. The modified backbone and sugars designed earlier are introduced as special building blocks during this assembly. The whole process runs under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|GMP]].

The same arithmetic of imperfection that haunts peptides applies here: each cycle is highly efficient but not perfect, so over roughly twenty cycles the small per-step failures pile up into shortened strands missing a letter, which are extremely similar to the full-length product. Purification — by chromatography that separates strands by length and charge — is therefore demanding and resource-heavy, and is one of the most expensive parts of the whole process.

The characteristic difficulty is scale and waste: phosphoramidite synthesis was invented for the tiny amounts research needs, but a successful oligonucleotide drug may require hundreds of kilograms — even a metric ton — and the process consumes huge volumes of solvent and large reagent excesses at that scale, driving high cost and pushing the industry toward continuous processing and enzymatic synthesis. The output is a purified oligonucleotide drug substance of defined sequence and chemistry, made to specification and ready for trials.
