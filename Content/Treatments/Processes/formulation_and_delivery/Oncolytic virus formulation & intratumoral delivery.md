The built oncolytic virus has to be made into a stable product and delivered where it can do its work — most often by injecting it straight into the tumor. Like a gene-therapy vector it is a fragile virus particle that loses the ability to infect if mishandled, so it is formulated in a buffered, stabilized solution and stored frozen, often very cold, to keep the live virus intact until use.

The distinctive feature is the delivery route: _intratumoral_ injection, directly into the visible tumor mass. This is deliberate. Putting the virus into the tumor concentrates it where it should replicate, limits how much reaches healthy tissue, and — crucially — keeps it out of the bloodstream, where the body's antiviral antibodies would neutralize it before it arrived. Talimogene laherparepvec, the first oncolytic virus approved in the US and Europe, is injected this way into melanoma deposits in the skin and lymph nodes. Formulating for this use brings its own constraints: the product must be deliverable in the small volume a tumor can hold, and stay stable as it is thawed and handled in the clinic.

The characteristic difficulty is reaching the cancer that matters. Intratumoral injection works only for tumors a clinician can actually get a needle into; the disease that kills — widespread internal metastases — is largely beyond reach this way, and giving the virus into the blood instead runs straight into pre-existing immunity that clears it. This delivery bottleneck, more than the virus's design, is what limits where the modality can be used today, and the hope is that a local injection still wakes a body-wide immune attack on distant tumors. The output is a stable, deliverable oncolytic virus product, ready for testing.
