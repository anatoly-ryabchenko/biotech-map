A purified antigen, given on its own, often provokes too weak an immune response to protect — the immune system needs a danger signal alongside it to react strongly and to remember. That signal is an [[Adjuvant|adjuvant]]: an added ingredient whose job is not to be recognized itself but to amplify and shape the response to the antigen. Choosing and formulating it is the work of this step. (mRNA and live-vector vaccines usually need no separate adjuvant, because the delivery itself already alarms the immune system.)

Adjuvants differ in strength and in the _kind_ of immunity they raise, and the choice is matched to the disease. The oldest, _alum_ (aluminium salts), grips the antigen and releases it slowly while mildly irritating the local tissue, favouring an antibody response — enough for many vaccines. Oil-in-water emulsions (such as MF59) recruit immune cells more vigorously. The most potent modern adjuvants combine ingredients that directly trip the immune system's pathogen sensors: AS01, used in the shingles vaccine, pairs a detoxified piece of bacterial surface molecule (which trips a specific sensor, TLR4) with a plant-derived saponin, the two together driving the strong, T-cell-rich response needed against harder targets.

Formulation then combines antigen and adjuvant into one stable, sterile, injectable product, making sure the antigen stays properly associated with the adjuvant and that the mixture survives storage.

The characteristic difficulty is the trade-off between power and tolerability: a stronger adjuvant gives better, longer protection but also more soreness, fever, and — rarely — the risk of overshooting into harmful inflammation. Because vaccines are given to vast numbers of _healthy_ people, often children, that safety bar is far higher than for a drug given to the sick, so the art is picking the weakest adjuvant that still protects. The output is a complete, adjuvanted, formulated vaccine, ready for preclinical immune testing.
