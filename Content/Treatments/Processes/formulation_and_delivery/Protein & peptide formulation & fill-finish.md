Proteins and peptides cannot be swallowed — stomach acid and gut enzymes would destroy them — so they are given by injection, and this step turns the purified molecule into a sterile, stable injectable sealed into its final container. It serves antibodies, designed proteins, therapeutic proteins, antibody–drug conjugates, and peptides, whose formulation problems are alike enough to share a step.

The core challenge is keeping a large, fragile molecule intact in a vial for months to years. The chief enemy is _aggregation_ — molecules clumping together — which saps potency and can provoke immune reactions; proteins are also attacked by slow chemical decay, by freezing and thawing, and by contact with surfaces and the air–water interface. The formulation is a carefully chosen mix of inactive ingredients (_excipients_) that hold these off: a buffer (often histidine) to fix the acidity, a sugar such as sucrose or trehalose to brace the folded shape, an amino acid like arginine to keep molecules apart, and a little _surfactant_ (polysorbate) to shield the protein at surfaces where it would otherwise unfold and stick.

Two physical forms are used. A liquid is simplest but less stable; for fragile molecules the solution is freeze-dried — see [[Lyophilization|lyophilization]] — into a dry cake reconstituted with water just before use, trading convenience for shelf life. A special difficulty for antibodies is that the dose is large (hundreds of milligrams), so they must be made very concentrated to fit a small injection volume — and concentrated protein solutions turn syrupy (_viscous_) and aggregate more readily, a problem partly headed off earlier in developability engineering.

_Fill-finish_ is the final, exacting act: the sterile drug is metered into vials, prefilled syringes, or pen cartridges, sealed, and kept absolutely free of contamination — a sterility failure here destroys a finished batch of an expensive biologic.

The characteristic difficulty is that the conditions that stabilize a protein (cold, dilute, undisturbed) conflict with what a usable medicine demands (concentrated, room-stable, easy to inject), so formulation is a balancing act with no perfect answer. The output is a sterile, stable, dosed injectable, ready for testing.
