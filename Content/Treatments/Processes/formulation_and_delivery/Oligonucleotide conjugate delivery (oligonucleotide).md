Unlike mRNA, a chemically stabilized oligonucleotide is small and tough enough to survive in the blood on its own — but it still faces the problem of getting into the right cells, since a bare strand is taken up poorly and indiscriminately. Rather than wrap it in a particle, this modality solves delivery by _conjugation_: bolting a targeting tag directly onto the oligonucleotide so the molecule delivers itself.

The dominant tag is [[GalNAc conjugation|GalNAc]], a small cluster of a sugar that a receptor found almost only on liver cells recognizes and pulls inside. Attaching GalNAc concentrates the drug in the liver and lets it be given as a simple injection under the skin, from which it drains and homes to the liver — turning what would be an infusion into a self-administered shot, in some cases needed only a few times a year. Because so many diseases are driven by proteins the liver makes, this one trick underlies most approved oligonucleotide drugs.

The work here is finalizing that conjugate as a drug product: confirming the targeting tag is attached correctly and stably, and formulating the molecule — usually just dissolved in a simple buffered salt solution, since these molecules are far more rugged than proteins or mRNA and need neither elaborate stabilization nor cold storage.

The characteristic difficulty is the flip side of the GalNAc trick: it works beautifully for the liver and almost nowhere else, because no comparably clean "doorway" receptor has been found for most other tissues. Reaching muscle, brain, or other organs — where many untreated diseases lie — is the field's central unsolved delivery problem, and conjugates aimed at them are far less efficient and still maturing. The output is a finished, deliverable oligonucleotide drug product, ready for testing.
