A cell therapy or regenerative product _is_ living cells, and living cells cannot sit on a shelf. They have to be preserved between the place they are made and the patient, and the only way to pause living cells for that journey is to deep-freeze them — _cryopreservation_ — and thaw them at the bedside.

Freezing is itself the danger. As water freezes, ice crystals form and tear cells apart, and the salt left behind in the shrinking unfrozen liquid concentrates to poisonous levels. Two measures prevent this. A _cryoprotectant_, usually DMSO at around 5–10%, is added; it soaks into the cells and blunts ice formation, letting them survive. And the cells are cooled slowly and precisely — typically about 1 °C per minute in a controlled-rate freezer — so water leaves the cells gradually instead of freezing inside them, before they go into liquid-nitrogen storage. Thawing is then done fast, at the bedside, to get back through the danger zone quickly.

The formulation is the medium the cells are frozen in: the cryoprotectant plus protective proteins (human serum albumin) and salts that keep the cells alive and whole. Unlike a molecule, the "dose" is a count of _live, functional_ cells, so the measure of success is viability and potency after thaw, not chemical purity.

The characteristic difficulty is that every cell product is precious and often singular — an autologous batch is the patient's only dose — yet cells are stressed at every step of freezing, storage, shipping, and thawing, and DMSO is itself toxic to cells (and unpleasant for patients) if they linger in it. A botched thaw at the hospital can destroy a therapy that took weeks to build. This is what drives the whole cold "vein-to-vein" chain that defines these modalities. The output is a frozen, viable, potency-tested cell product, ready for testing and, ultimately, infusion.
