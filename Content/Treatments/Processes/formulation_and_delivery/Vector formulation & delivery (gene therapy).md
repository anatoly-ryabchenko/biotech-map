The engineered viral vector carrying the therapeutic gene has to be turned into a stable, sterile product that can be injected and that arrives at its target tissue with its delicate cargo intact. The vector is a protein shell wrapped around DNA, and like other biologics it is fragile — prone to clumping, to sticking to surfaces, and to losing the ability to infect.

Formulation puts the vector into a buffered salt solution at near-neutral acidity, with stabilizers (often a sugar such as sucrose) to protect it through the freezing and thawing it will undergo. Because no liquid form stays good for long, the product is generally stored deep-frozen, below about −60 °C, where it keeps for a long time. The acidity and saltiness (osmolality) are controlled tightly, because the vector is often delivered into delicate places — the eye, the fluid around the spinal cord, the brain — that tolerate little deviation.

The other half of this step is the _delivery route_, which is part of the product's design. A vector can be given into the bloodstream to reach a widespread tissue such as liver or muscle — which demands an enormous dose, since most is lost on the way — or injected locally, into the eye or spinal fluid or a specific organ, to concentrate it where it is needed and lower both the dose and the immune exposure.

The characteristic difficulty is a contamination problem peculiar to this modality: vector preparations contain a large fraction of _empty shells_ — capsids that never packaged the gene — which add to the immune burden without adding benefit, and which formulation and the upstream process can reduce but not fully remove. Together with the very high doses needed for whole-body delivery, this makes the immune reaction to the vector itself a dose-limiting hazard. The output is a sterile, frozen, deliverable vector product, ready for testing.
