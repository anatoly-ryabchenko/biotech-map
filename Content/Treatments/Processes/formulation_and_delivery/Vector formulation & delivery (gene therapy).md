The engineered viral vector carrying the therapeutic gene has to be turned into a stable, sterile product that can be injected and that arrives at its target tissue with its delicate cargo intact. The vector is a protein shell wrapped around DNA, and like other biologics it is fragile — prone to clumping, to sticking to surfaces, and to losing the ability to infect.

Formulation puts the vector into a buffered salt solution at near-neutral acidity, with stabilizers (often a sugar such as sucrose) to protect it through the freezing and thawing it will undergo. Because no liquid form stays good for long, the product is generally stored deep-frozen, below about −60 °C, where it keeps for a long time. The acidity and saltiness (osmolality) are controlled tightly, because the vector is often delivered into delicate places — the eye, the fluid around the spinal cord, the brain — that tolerate little deviation.

The other half of this step is the _delivery route_, which is part of the product's design. A vector can be given into the bloodstream to reach a widespread tissue such as liver or muscle — which demands an enormous dose, since most is lost on the way — or injected locally, into the eye or spinal fluid or a specific organ, to concentrate it where it is needed and lower both the dose and the immune exposure.

For AAV, the characteristic difficulty is a contamination problem peculiar to this modality: vector preparations contain a large fraction of _empty shells_ — capsids that never packaged the gene — which add to the immune burden without adding benefit, and which formulation and the upstream process can reduce but not fully remove. Together with the very high doses needed for whole-body delivery, this makes the reaction to the vector itself a dose-limiting hazard — one that has proved fatal: in 2025, acute liver failure after high-dose, whole-body AAV dosing killed patients treated with one company's muscle-targeted vector. The output is a sterile, frozen, deliverable vector product, ready for testing.

In-body CAR-T (the gene-therapy variant in which the patient's own T cells are engineered inside the body) is formulated and delivered differently from the AAV described above. Its lentiviral particle is wrapped in a fragile fatty membrane, not a hard protein shell, and carries its T-cell binder on that membrane, so the product is kept deep-frozen and thawed as few times as possible; damage to the membrane or the binder takes away the ability to find T cells. The route is a single infusion into a vein, where the T cells circulate. It replaces the cell-therapy sequence of collecting cells, waiting weeks for manufacturing, and giving lymphodepleting chemotherapy first; the first trials gave it without that chemotherapy. Doses are counted in _transducing units_ — only the particles that actually deliver their gene: Kelonia's KLN-1010 has used levels from 4 × 10⁶ to 2 × 10⁷ transducing units per kilogram, roughly 10⁹ for a 70 kg adult. The characteristic difficulty is that nobody can count the engineered cells that result. The particles that happen to find T cells decide how many CAR-T cells exist, and those then multiply on their own, so the amount varies between patients: across the first patients given KLN-1010, CAR-carrying T cells made up between 22% and 85% of the T cells circulating in the blood by day 15 — the highest figure at the lowest dose, not the highest — and two of those patients were given the steroid dexamethasone to calm the resulting surge of lymphocytes. The same immune reaction as in conventional CAR-T, _cytokine release syndrome_ (fever and low blood pressure from signalling molecules released by activated T cells), occurs: in LB2501's early trial it affected two-thirds of patients (8 of 12), almost all at grade 1, the mildest of five grades, with no patient needing steroids for it; reactions to the infusion itself affected 75%. Programmes that use lipid nanoparticles carrying mRNA instead give the receptor only briefly, which makes the dose easier to stop but not to count.
