mRNA and in-body gene-editing payloads share a fatal weakness: they are large, fragile genetic strands that blood enzymes destroy within minutes and that cannot cross into cells on their own. The solution — the thing that finally made these modalities work — is to wrap them in a [[Lipid nanoparticle|lipid nanoparticle]] (LNP), a tiny fatty sphere that hides the cargo and carries it into cells.

An LNP is built from four kinds of fat in set proportions. The key one is an _ionizable lipid_: electrically neutral in the blood, which keeps the particle safe and non-toxic, but turning positively charged in the acidic pocket inside a cell that swallows it — and that charge switch is what springs the cargo loose. The other three round out the shell: a helper phospholipid, cholesterol for rigidity, and a trace of a PEG-coated lipid that sets the particle's size and how long it circulates.

Making the particle is a physical process. The lipids (dissolved in alcohol) and the genetic strands (in water) are forced together through a microfluidic mixer — a channel with a tortuous path that blends them in milliseconds — so the lipids snap shut around the cargo, trapping it inside at high efficiency.

The hardest part of delivery is the last step, _endosomal escape_. After a cell engulfs the LNP, the particle is trapped in a bubble (an _endosome_) that would normally digest it. As that bubble acidifies, the ionizable lipid charges up, disrupts the bubble's membrane, and lets the cargo slip out into the cell's interior where it can work. This escape is strikingly inefficient — often only a small percent of the cargo gets out — and improving it is a central research problem.

The characteristic difficulty is twofold: LNPs given into the blood naturally pile up in the liver, so reaching any other organ is hard and limits which diseases these modalities can treat; and the particles are fragile, often needing deep-freeze storage — the main reason the first mRNA vaccines demanded ultra-cold logistics. The output is the genetic payload safely packaged in LNPs and deliverable into cells, ready for testing.
