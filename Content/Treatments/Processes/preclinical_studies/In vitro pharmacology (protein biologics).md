This step tests antibodies, computationally designed proteins, and therapeutic proteins in living cells, confirming each does its specific job. Because these three act differently, the assays differ. An antibody is checked for binding its target with the right strength and specificity and for the functional consequence — neutralizing a signal, or, for many cancer antibodies, recruiting immune killing of the cell it has bound (effector functions such as antibody-dependent cell killing, tested in co-cultures of antibody, target cell, and immune cells). A designed protein or a therapeutic protein is checked for its intended activity — an enzyme converting its [[Substrate|substrate]], a hormone or trap engaging its receptor, a binder blocking its target.

Two cross-cutting tests prepare for the animal studies that follow. _Specificity_ is probed hard, including _tissue cross-reactivity_ — exposing the biologic to a panel of human tissues to see whether it sticks anywhere unexpected, an early warning of off-target toxicity. And _species cross-reactivity_ is measured: whether the biologic also binds the equivalent target in rats or monkeys, which decides whether those animals are even usable for safety testing, since a fully human-specific biologic may engage no animal target at all. The biologic's [[Immunogenicity|immunogenicity]] risk is also assessed.

The characteristic difficulty is precisely this species gap: a biologic exquisitely specific for the human target may not recognize any animal's version, so the standard safety species are blind to its action — forcing reliance on a single cross-reactive species, a surrogate molecule, or specially engineered animals. The output is in-cell confirmation of binding, function, and specificity, plus the species data that shape all the preclinical testing to come.
