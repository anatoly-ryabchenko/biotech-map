For mRNA and oligonucleotide drugs, the whole-animal test centres on a question the dish could not answer: did the drug reach the right tissue? Both modalities are dominated by _delivery_, so [[ADME|pharmacokinetics]] here is less about blood levels than about _biodistribution_ — where in the body the drug actually went. For a [[Lipid nanoparticle|LNP]]-wrapped mRNA, that means confirming it reached and expressed its protein in the target tissue, and quantifying how much piled up in the liver, the default destination. For a [[GalNAc conjugation|GalNAc]]-tagged oligonucleotide, it means confirming liver uptake and measuring how deeply and how long the target gene stays knocked down — often months from a single dose.

Efficacy is tested in disease models where available, reading out the intended molecular effect — a lowered disease protein, a corrected message, an expressed therapeutic protein — and its consequence. The unusually long duration of oligonucleotide effects, and the brief burst of mRNA expression, both shape the dosing schedule worked out here.

The characteristic difficulty is that delivery is the whole game and it does not transfer from the dish: a sequence potent in cultured cells is useless if, in the animal, it never leaves the bloodstream or reaches only the liver when the disease lies elsewhere. This is why so much rides on these in-vivo distribution studies, and why both modalities are largely confined today to diseases of the liver and a few other reachable tissues. The output is evidence of where the drug goes, what it does there, and for how long — ready for safety testing.
