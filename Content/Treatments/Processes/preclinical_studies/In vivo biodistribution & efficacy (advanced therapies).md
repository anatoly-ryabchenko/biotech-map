For the advanced therapies — gene therapy, gene editing, engineered cells, regenerative cells, and oncolytic viruses — a central preclinical question is simply: where did the medicine go, and did it stay where it should? This _biodistribution_ study tracks the cells, vector, or virus through the animal's organs over time, because these living or self-replicating medicines can travel, lodge, persist, or multiply in places they were never meant to. A gene-therapy vector is traced to see which tissues took up the gene — and whether any reached the gonads, raising the spectre of heritable changes; engineered or regenerative cells are tracked to confirm they home to and engraft in the right place and nowhere else; an oncolytic virus is followed to see whether it stays in the tumour or _sheds_ into the body and the environment.

Alongside distribution, _efficacy_ is tested in animal disease models — correction of a genetic defect, regression of a tumour, replacement of lost cells — though this is often constrained by the same species barriers seen across biologics: a human-specific vector or a human cell graft may need immune-deficient or "humanized" animals to study at all.

The characteristic difficulty is _persistence_: unlike an ordinary drug that clears, these medicines can last for years or for life — an integrated gene, a permanent edit, long-lived engrafted cells — so a problem seeded now may surface far later, and a short animal study cannot see it. Distribution to the wrong tissue, or unexpected long-term presence, is exactly what the safety step that follows must probe. The output is a map of where the therapy travels and persists, plus efficacy evidence, leading into specialized safety testing.
