The optimized peptide is tested in living cells to confirm it does its intended job and to expose weaknesses before animals. Most peptide drugs work by engaging a receptor on the cell surface — switching it on (an _agonist_) or blocking it (an _antagonist_) — so the core test is a cell-based functional [[Assay|assay]] that measures this action and the concentration at which it occurs (the potency), alongside binding strength and selectivity against related receptors.

A test that matters more for peptides than for small molecules is _plasma stability_: the peptide is incubated in blood serum to see how quickly the body's protein-cutting enzymes chop it up — a direct check on whether the stabilizing modifications added during optimization actually hold. Where the target sits inside the cell, uptake is checked too.

The characteristic difficulty is the gap between a clean receptor assay and the body: a peptide can be a potent, selective agonist in a dish yet be cut apart within minutes in real serum, or fold differently once among the membranes and proteins of a living cell and lose the shape it was designed to hold. Only peptides that combine genuine potency with genuine stability advance. The output is in-cell confirmation of functional potency, selectivity, and serum stability.
