Before a person can be given the drug, regulators require formal proof that its dangers have been mapped. This is the job of GLP toxicology: a battery of animal safety studies run under _Good Laboratory Practice_ — a strict, auditable quality system in which every procedure is pre-specified, every measurement recorded, and every step traceable, so the results are trustworthy enough to license a human trial. It is the gate to first-in-human testing, shared by all the conventional modalities here.

The core is _repeat-dose toxicity_ testing in two species — one rodent (usually rat) and one non-rodent (often dog or monkey) — given the drug for weeks to months at several dose levels, then examined exhaustively: blood chemistry, organ weights, and microscopic study of every tissue, to find what the drug harms, at what dose, and whether the harm reverses. The key number extracted is the _NOAEL_, the highest dose producing no adverse effect. Layered on are _safety pharmacology_ studies of the vital systems — above all the heart's rhythm, plus lungs and brain — and _genotoxicity_ tests of whether the drug damages DNA, a cancer warning.

From the NOAEL a safe human _starting dose_ is calculated: the animal dose is scaled to human size and divided by a safety factor, often tenfold or more. For drugs that could over-stimulate the immune system, a more cautious anchor is used instead — the lowest dose expected to have any biological effect at all (MABEL) — to avoid a dangerous over-reaction in the first humans.

Regulators are now working to shrink this animal work. In 2025 the FDA set out a plan to replace much of it — starting with antibodies, then other drugs — with _new approach methodologies_: tests on human cells and on miniature lab-grown organ tissue (_organoids_), plus computer models of toxicity. One early step is letting a six-month monkey study be skipped when a one-month study raises no concern; for now, though, the two-species battery remains the norm.

The characteristic difficulty is that animals do not perfectly predict human toxicity: some human-specific harms never appear in any animal, while animals sometimes show toxicities irrelevant to people — so the safety margins are kept deliberately conservative to cover what the studies might miss. The output is a documented safety profile and a justified safe starting dose — the right to begin human trials — after which production is scaled up under GMP to supply the trials.
