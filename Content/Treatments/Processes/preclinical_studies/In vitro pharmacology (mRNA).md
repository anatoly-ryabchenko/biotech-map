Here the question is whether the packaged mRNA actually makes its protein once inside a cell, and whether it does so quietly. Cells are exposed to the [[Lipid nanoparticle|LNP]]-wrapped mRNA and three things are measured. _Expression_: how much of the encoded protein the cells produce, and for how long — mRNA acts transiently, and output fades as the strand decays. _Function and identity_: that the protein the cell makes is the correct, properly folded, working version — a viral antigen in its right shape for a vaccine, an active enzyme for a replacement therapy. _Innate immune activation_: whether the foreign RNA still trips the cell's antiviral alarms despite the [[Modified nucleosides|modified nucleosides]] built in, which would shut down protein production and, for a protein-replacement use, cause unwanted inflammation.

Uptake and expression are read out together, because the [[Lipid nanoparticle|lipid nanoparticle]] and the mRNA succeed or fail as a unit — these assays test the finished particle, not the bare strand.

The characteristic difficulty is the tug-of-war between output and quietness: pushing for more protein can mean more of the double-stranded RNA contaminant, or sequence features that trip the alarms, and a construct that expresses beautifully in one cell type may barely work in another that takes up LNPs poorly. For a vaccine a measure of immune activation is welcome; for a replacement protein it is purely harmful — so "good" is defined by the use. The output is in-cell confirmation that the delivered mRNA expresses a functional protein with acceptable immune stimulation.
