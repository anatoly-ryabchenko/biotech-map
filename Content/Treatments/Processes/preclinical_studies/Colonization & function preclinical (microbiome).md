For a live biotherapeutic the preclinical question is whether the bacteria can take up residence in the gut and do their intended job there. The central tool is the _germ-free_ (or _gnotobiotic_) mouse: an animal raised in complete isolation with no microbes of its own, into which the candidate strains can be introduced cleanly. Because there is no resident community to compete with or to muddy the picture, these animals let researchers see plainly whether the strains _colonize_ — establish and persist in the gut rather than washing straight through — and whether they perform their function (producing the intended molecule, displacing a pathogen, easing inflammation in a disease model).

Models closer to reality are also used: mice given antibiotics to clear space, or "humanized" mice carrying a transplanted human microbial community, to test whether the strains can engraft against existing competition — far harder than colonizing an empty gut.

The characteristic difficulty is that a mouse gut is not a human gut: it differs in chemistry, diet, and resident species, so a strain that colonizes a germ-free mouse beautifully may fail to gain a foothold in a person whose gut is already full, and a function shown in a mouse disease model may not carry over. Engraftment against an intact human community — the real clinical situation — is the crux and the least faithfully modelled. The output is evidence that the strains colonize and function in a living gut, leading to manufacturing.
