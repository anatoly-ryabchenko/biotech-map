An antibody–drug conjugate must do something an ordinary antibody need not: kill the cell it binds. The defining test is therefore _target-dependent killing_ — the conjugate is placed on cells that carry the target marker and on cells that lack it, and should destroy the first while sparing the second, proving the killing is aimed rather than indiscriminate. The strength of this killing is measured as a dose-response.

The assays also dissect the mechanism the conjugate depends on: that the antibody, once bound, is _internalized_ into the cell; that the linker then releases the payload inside; and that the freed payload kills. A further effect is checked — the _bystander effect_, where payload released in one cell leaks into and kills neighbouring tumour cells that lack the marker, which can help against patchy tumours but also hints at a route to harming healthy neighbours.

The characteristic difficulty is that the conjugate's behaviour in a dish only partly predicts its behaviour in blood: the linker that looks stable in culture may slowly shed payload in the circulation (tested more fully in the step that follows), and a too-strong bystander effect blurs the very selectivity the modality is built on. Cell assays catch aimed killing and mechanism; they cannot fully catch premature release. The output is in-cell confirmation of target-dependent killing and the internalize–release–kill mechanism.
