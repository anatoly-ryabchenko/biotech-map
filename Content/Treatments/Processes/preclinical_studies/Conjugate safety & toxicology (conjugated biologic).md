An antibody–drug conjugate needs its own formal safety studies, because its toxicity follows rules a plain antibody's does not. Run under the same audited _Good Laboratory Practice_ standard as other modalities, these two-species studies focus on the harms specific to a targeted poison: damage caused by the payload coming loose in the body, and damage to healthy tissues that happen to carry a trace of the target marker.

In practice a conjugate's dose-limiting toxicities are usually _off-target_ and _payload-driven_ — the freed toxin harms fast-dividing tissues (bone marrow, gut lining, and sometimes liver, nerves, or eyes) regardless of where the antibody was aimed. Because of this, different conjugates that share a payload class often show similar toxicities, appearing at doses unrelated to the target. The studies map which organs are hit, at what dose, and whether the damage reverses, and they set the safe human starting dose. Since the payload is so potent, the gap between an effective dose and a toxic one is often narrow, which makes this assessment especially decisive.

The characteristic difficulty is attributing harm correctly: when a tissue is damaged, is it because it carried a little of the target, because free payload reached it, or because the whole conjugate was taken up non-specifically? Untangling these decides whether to fix the linker, the payload, or the antibody — and a species whose target distribution differs from a human's can mislead about which organs are truly at risk. The output is a documented, payload-aware safety profile and a safe starting dose, leading to conjugate manufacturing.
