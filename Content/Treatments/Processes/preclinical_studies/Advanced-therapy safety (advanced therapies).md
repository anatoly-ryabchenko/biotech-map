The advanced therapies raise safety questions no conventional drug does, because they are permanent, living, or self-replicating — so their formal safety assessment is specialized and dominated by long-term hazards. The overriding concern is _cancer_. For gene therapies and edited cells the danger is [[Insertional mutagenesis|insertional mutagenesis]]: a delivered gene or an integrating vector landing in the genome near a growth-controlling gene and switching it on, seeding a tumour years later — the risk that famously caused leukaemias in early gene-therapy trials. For regenerative and cell products the parallel danger is leftover dividing cells forming tumours; for any edit, the consequences of cutting at unintended sites.

Other modality-specific hazards are probed: whether engineered or viral medicines provoke dangerous _immune reactions_ — including the violent, body-wide immune surge a cell therapy can trigger — whether an oncolytic virus or vector _persists or sheds_ to others, and whether the therapy can be switched off if it goes wrong. Because these effects can unfold over a lifetime, animal studies are stretched as long as is feasible and paired with the most sensitive available assays — yet they still cannot span the decades a permanent therapy will act over.

The characteristic difficulty is exactly this mismatch of timescale and species: the worst risks — a cancer from one mis-placed gene, a delayed immune event — are rare, slow, and may have no animal counterpart, so they cannot be ruled out before humans, only minimized and then watched. This is why these modalities carry mandatory years-long follow-up into the clinic. The output is a long-term-hazard safety assessment that, with its limits openly acknowledged, supports cautious first human use — leading into manufacturing.
