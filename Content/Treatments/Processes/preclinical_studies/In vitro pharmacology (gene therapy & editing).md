This step tests, in human cells, whether the genetic medicine does its molecular job. For a gene therapy that means confirming the vector enters the intended cells (_transduction_), the delivered gene is read, and enough functional protein is produced to correct the defect — measured as expression level plus a functional readout, such as a missing enzyme's activity restored. For gene editing it means confirming the editing machinery reaches the target site and makes the intended change at a useful rate (the _editing efficiency_, the percentage of gene copies corrected), and that the correction restores normal function.

Both are tested where possible in the very human cell type the therapy will treat — or in patient-derived cells carrying the real disease mutation — because behaviour is strongly cell-dependent. (The exhaustive hunt for editing _off-target_ activity was done in optimization; here the focus is on-target correction and its functional payoff.)

The characteristic difficulty is that cultured cells take up vectors and editors far more readily than cells in a living body, and dividing cells in a dish differ from the quiet, mature cells of real tissue — so an efficiency that looks ample here can collapse in vivo, where the vector must also survive the bloodstream and the immune system to arrive. The dish proves the mechanism works; it cannot prove it will reach enough cells in a person. The output is in-cell confirmation of transduction or editing efficiency and functional correction.
