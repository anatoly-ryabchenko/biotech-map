The engineered cells are themselves the drug, so "pharmacology" here means testing what the cells do when they meet their target. The central assay is a _co-culture_: the engineered T cells are mixed with target-bearing cells and, separately, with target-free cells, and several things measured — how efficiently they kill the target cells (potency); that they ignore the target-free cells (specificity, the safety-critical readout); how much immune-signalling protein (cytokines) they release; and whether they multiply in response to the target.

Longer-term qualities are probed too: whether the cells keep killing through repeated exposure to target, or instead become _exhausted_ — a worn-out, ineffective state that is a leading reason cell therapies stop working. The cells' identity and the amount of engineered receptor on their surface are confirmed.

The characteristic difficulty is that a dish cannot reproduce the body the cells must work in: real tumours wrap themselves in signals that suppress T cells, hide in tissues the cells must travel to, and differ from patient to patient — so cells that kill briskly in co-culture may stall inside a living tumour. And the most dangerous failure — attacking a healthy tissue that happens to share the target — can only be partly seen here, since not every tissue is present in the dish. The output is in-vitro confirmation of potent, specific, durable killing and a cytokine profile, before the cells are tested in animals.
