This is the first test of the drug in a whole living animal, and it answers two questions at once: does it actually treat the disease, and what does the body do to it? _Efficacy_ is tested in an animal _disease model_ — animals in which the disease, or something resembling it, has been induced or bred (a tumour implanted, a gene mutated) — by giving the drug and measuring whether the disease eases. _[[ADME|Pharmacokinetics]]_ is measured in parallel: blood samples taken over time show how high the drug's concentration climbs, how long it persists, and how fast it is cleared (its half-life), so the dose and dosing schedule can be worked out.

The two are linked into a _PK/PD_ picture — connecting the drug's concentration in the body (pharmacokinetics) to its biological effect (pharmacodynamics) — so the team learns what blood level is needed to work and for how long it must be held, the basis for predicting a human dose. Degraders add a wrinkle: their effect (the protein staying degraded) can persist long after the drug itself has cleared, so the action outlasts the exposure.

The characteristic difficulty is that animal disease models are imperfect mirrors of human disease — a tumour in a mouse is not a human cancer, and a model that responds can still mislead — which is a major reason drugs that work here later fail in patients. And a compound can be efficacious yet have poor pharmacokinetics (cleared too fast to dose practically), or the reverse, forcing hard go/no-go calls. The output is evidence the drug works in a living body and a quantitative grasp of its pharmacokinetics, ready for formal safety testing.
