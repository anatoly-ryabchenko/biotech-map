A radiopharmaceutical's preclinical work answers a question unique to radiation drugs: not only "does it reach the tumour?" but "how much radiation dose does every organ receive?" Animals carrying the target are given the radioligand and then imaged or dissected over time to build a _biodistribution_ picture — confirming the drug accumulates in the target tissue, and measuring how much lands in, and how long it lingers in, each healthy organ.

Those distribution data feed _dosimetry_: a calculation that turns "how much radioactivity sat in an organ, and for how long" into the _absorbed radiation dose_ that organ receives, accounting for the kind of radiation the isotope emits — short-range, intense alpha particles versus longer-reaching beta particles. Dosimetry is what converts a biodistribution map into a safety statement, and it focuses on the organs known to limit these therapies: above all the bone marrow (highly radiation-sensitive) and the kidneys (which often clear these molecules and so are irradiated as they do). These animal estimates give the first projection of human organ doses, required before any clinical use.

The characteristic difficulty is the ticking, indiscriminate nature of the agent: radiation harms whatever it sits near, so a molecule that clears slowly or sticks to a healthy organ delivers dose there regardless of how well it targets, and the harm is cumulative and partly delayed. Small animals also scale imperfectly to humans for dose, so the estimates are approximate and deliberately cautious. The output is a biodistribution and dosimetry profile with projected human organ doses and a radiation-safety assessment, leading to manufacturing and radiolabeling.
