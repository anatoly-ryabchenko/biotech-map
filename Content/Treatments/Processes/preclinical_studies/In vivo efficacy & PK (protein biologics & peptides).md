This step gives antibodies, designed proteins, therapeutic proteins, and peptides to whole animals to test whether they treat the disease and how the body handles them. Efficacy is read out in an animal _disease model_; [[ADME|pharmacokinetics]] is followed from blood samples — but the story is very different from a small molecule's. These are large molecules given by injection, so there is no oral-absorption question; instead the key facts are how long they last (antibodies can persist for weeks, rescued from breakdown by a recycling system in the blood, while an unmodified peptide may last only minutes) and how they distribute (large proteins stay mostly in the bloodstream and struggle to reach inside tissues).

A complication special to biologics shapes these studies: many are exquisitely specific to the _human_ target and do not engage the rat or monkey version, so a standard animal may show no effect at all — not because the drug fails, but because the animal has nothing for it to act on. Teams work around this with specially engineered animals, surrogate molecules made against the animal's own target, or the one species that happens to cross-react. The animal's [[Immunogenicity|immune response]] to the foreign protein is watched too, since anti-drug antibodies can neutralize it or speed its clearance mid-study.

The characteristic difficulty is this species mismatch layered on top of imperfect disease models: an effect, or its absence, in an animal may say more about whether the animal's biology matches the drug than about how it will behave in patients. The output is evidence of efficacy and a pharmacokinetic profile (half-life, distribution, dosing), ready for safety testing.
