The core test is simple to state: does the oligonucleotide knock down — or redirect — its target message inside living cells, and only that message? Cells are dosed across a range of concentrations and the level of the target message (or the protein it codes for) is measured, giving a dose-response curve and a potency. For a gene-silencing oligonucleotide the readout is how far the target falls; for a splice-switching one, whether the cell now makes the corrected version of the message.

Selectivity is checked by surveying the whole cell's messages (by sequencing) for other genes that dropped — the off-target silencing that the seed-region tuning in optimization was meant to prevent. Because chemistry governs everything for this modality, the assays also confirm the modified strand is taken up by cells and reaches the compartment where its machinery works.

The characteristic difficulty is that bare oligonucleotides enter cultured cells poorly, so labs often force them in artificially to read potency — which can flatter a molecule that, left to reach cells on its own, would never get to its target in a living body. This "free uptake versus assisted uptake" gap means an in-dish potency can mislead, and true delivery is only settled in animals. Selectivity, by contrast, transfers well from this step. The output is in-cell confirmation of potent, selective knockdown or splice correction of the target message.
