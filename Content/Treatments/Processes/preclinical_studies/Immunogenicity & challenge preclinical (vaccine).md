A vaccine's preclinical test is unlike a drug's, because success means provoking the immune system, not sparing a target. Two questions are asked in animals. _Immunogenicity_: does the vaccine raise the intended immune response? Animals are vaccinated and their blood tested for antibodies against the [[Antigen|antigen]] — crucially for _neutralizing_ antibodies, the ones that actually block the pathogen, measured as a _titre_ (how far the blood can be diluted and still neutralize) — and for the T-cell response where that matters. This reveals whether the antigen, adjuvant, and dose chosen upstream truly work together, and guides the schedule of priming and booster shots.

_Challenge_ is the decisive test. Vaccinated animals, and unvaccinated controls, are deliberately exposed to the live pathogen to see whether vaccination actually prevents infection or disease. This also helps establish a _correlate of protection_ — a measurable immune level (often a neutralizing-antibody titre) that predicts protection — which becomes invaluable later, since it lets trials and routine batch testing rely on a blood measurement instead of waiting for natural exposure.

The characteristic difficulty is that animals are imperfect stand-ins for human immunity and human disease: a pathogen may not infect an animal the same way, or at all; the animal's immune system may respond differently to the antigen; and a challenge model can be artificially harsh or mild — so a strong animal result is encouraging but not decisive. When the response is too weak or protection fails, the work loops back to redesign the antigen or adjuvant. The output is evidence the vaccine raises a protective response, with a candidate correlate of protection, leading to manufacturing.
