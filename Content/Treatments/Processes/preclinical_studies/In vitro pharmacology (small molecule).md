Before any animal sees the drug, the optimized compound is tested in living cells — a far more realistic setting than the isolated-protein assays of discovery, because here the compound must enter a real, crowded cell, find its target among thousands of other proteins, and produce the intended effect. This step asks three questions in order.

_Does it engage the target inside a cell?_ Target-engagement assays confirm the compound actually binds its target within an intact cell — for instance by measuring how binding changes the target protein's resistance to heat — not merely in a purified tube. _Does engaging it do the intended thing?_ A cell-based functional [[Assay|assay]] checks that hitting the target produces the wanted change — a signalling pathway switched off, a cancer cell stopped from dividing — and measures the concentration at which half the effect occurs (the cellular potency, an IC50 or EC50). _Does it hit only what it should?_ The compound is run against broad _selectivity panels_ — banks of related proteins (such as the dozens of related kinases) and unrelated ones known to cause side effects — to catch off-target activity early.

In parallel, the first real [[ADME|ADME]] measurements are made in cells and tissue fractions: whether the compound crosses cell membranes, how fast liver enzymes break it down, and whether it blocks the enzymes that clear other drugs (a source of dangerous drug–drug interactions).

The characteristic difficulty is the leap from a clean biochemical assay to a messy cell: a compound potent against the isolated protein often loses activity in cells because it cannot get in, is pumped back out, or is metabolized — and an apparent effect may come from general toxicity rather than true target action, which the engagement and selectivity assays exist to expose. Compounds that pass become the ones taken into animals. The output is in-cell confirmation of potency, mechanism, and selectivity.
