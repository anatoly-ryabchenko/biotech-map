For a regenerative product the question is whether the manufactured cells are truly the cell type they are meant to replace, and whether they behave like it. The assays test _identity_ — do the cells carry the molecular markers of, say, an insulin-making pancreatic cell or a dopamine neuron? — and _function_ — do they do that cell's job, secreting insulin in response to glucose, firing like a neuron, producing the right molecules? _Purity_ is quantified: what fraction of the batch is the intended type versus other cell types that arose during differentiation.

The overriding test, unique to this modality, is screening for _residual undifferentiated cells_ — leftover pluripotent stem cells that never differentiated. Even a tiny number is dangerous, because they keep the power to divide endlessly and can form a tumour after transplant, so highly sensitive assays hunt for their molecular signatures and the product must fall below a strict limit.

The characteristic difficulty is _maturity_: cells made in a few weeks of differentiation often resemble a fetal version of the target cell rather than the adult one, working only partially — a beta-like cell that releases insulin sluggishly, a neuron that has not fully wired. A dish cannot supply the long, complex cues the body uses to finish maturation, so the function measured here may over- or under-state what the cells will do once transplanted. The output is confirmation of cell identity, function, and purity, and clearance on residual stem cells, before animal testing.
