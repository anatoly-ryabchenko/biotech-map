The engineered virus is tested in cells for the two behaviours it was built to have. _Selective replication and killing_: the virus is placed on cancer cells of the intended type and on normal cells, and should multiply in and burst the cancer cells while barely growing in the normal ones — confirming the tumour-selectivity engineered through gene deletions. How strongly it kills, and how much new virus it makes, are measured across cell types.

The second behaviour is _immune provocation_. A key part of how these viruses work is that bursting a tumour cell in the right way spills danger signals that alert the immune system, so the assays look for the markers of this "immunogenic" cell death and confirm the virus makes the immune-recruiting protein (such as GM-CSF) it was armed with.

The characteristic difficulty is that a flat dish of one cell type cannot show the things that decide success inside a real tumour: whether the virus can physically spread through dense, three-dimensional tumour tissue, and whether the immune response it provokes mostly helps (attacking the cancer) or hurts (clearing the virus before it works). Selectivity and direct killing transfer reasonably from the dish; spread and the immune interplay do not, and must wait for animal models. The output is in-cell confirmation of tumour-selective replication, killing, and immune activation.
