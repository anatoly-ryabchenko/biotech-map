An antibody–drug conjugate is really three things travelling together — the antibody, the linker, and the toxic payload — and in a living body they can come apart. This step follows all three over time and begins to map the safety consequences. Blood samples are analyzed not just for "the conjugate" but separately for the intact conjugate, the bare antibody that has lost its payload, and free payload circulating on its own — because the gaps between these reveal how stable the linker really is in blood.

That stability is the crux. A linker that sheds its payload prematurely in the circulation releases free toxin into the body, which is the main source of an antibody–drug conjugate's off-target toxicity: damage to tissues that never carried the target marker at all — typically fast-dividing tissues like bone marrow and gut lining, which any cell poison harms. The studies measure how much free payload appears and begin to connect it to early signs of such damage, informing whether the linker or the drug-to-antibody ratio needs rethinking.

The characteristic difficulty is that a conjugate's toxicity is often driven not by the antibody's target but by the payload coming loose, so a beautifully targeted antibody can still poison the body if its linker leaks — and this becomes visible only in a whole organism with a real circulation and real clearance, not in cells. The output is a picture of the conjugate's stability and clearance and an early read on payload-driven toxicity, leading into formal conjugate safety testing.
