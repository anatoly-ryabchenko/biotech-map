A drug that has passed its trials still cannot be sold. An independent government regulator — the FDA in the United States, the EMA in Europe, and their counterparts elsewhere — must first review all the evidence and grant permission. Every modality passes through here.

The work is to assemble everything the program has learned into one vast submission (a New Drug Application for a small molecule, a Biologics License Application for a biologic) and then to work with the regulator toward a yes or no. The dossier pulls together three strands: the _clinical_ evidence of benefit and safety from the human trials; the _nonclinical_ science — pharmacology and the animal safety studies — that justified testing in people; and the _CMC_ package (Chemistry, Manufacturing, and Controls), a detailed account of how the drug is made and kept pure and consistent batch to batch. Regulators scrutinize manufacturing closely under [[Good Manufacturing Practice|Good Manufacturing Practice]], because a drug is only as safe as the batches actually produced, and they inspect the factories in person.

Review is a months-long back-and-forth of detailed questions and requested analyses — target decision times run about six to ten months — sometimes aided by an _advisory committee_ of outside experts. A central output is the _label_: the negotiated, official statement of exactly which patients the drug is approved for, at what dose, and with what warnings. For serious diseases lacking good treatments, an _accelerated approval_ may be granted on the strength of a [[Clinical endpoint|surrogate endpoint]] likely to predict benefit, with full confirmation required afterward.

Two kinds of medicine carry a **specialized layer on top of this general review**. The _advanced therapies_ — gene therapies, gene-edited products, engineered cells, regenerative cells, oncolytic viruses — are treated as a distinct class (overseen in Europe by a dedicated Committee for Advanced Therapies, and in the US through regenerative-medicine designations such as RMAT). Because they are permanent and made in tiny, complex, often one-per-patient batches, regulators scrutinize _manufacturing consistency_ far harder than usual — demanding extensive _comparability_ evidence whenever the process changes — and they make years-long post-approval follow-up a binding condition of the licence, since the worst risks (a delayed cancer) cannot be ruled out beforehand. _Radiopharmaceuticals_ carry a different added layer: because they are radioactive, every facility that makes, stores, labels, or administers the dose must also be _licensed_ to handle radioactive material (in the US, under the Nuclear Regulatory Commission and state authorities), with rules for shielding, waste, transport of short-lived isotopes, and releasing a briefly-radioactive patient — so the drug's reach depends on having enough appropriately licensed sites.

The characteristic difficulty is that approval turns on a judgement, not a formula: the regulator weighs an imperfect, never-complete evidence package and must balance the harm of approving something unsafe against the harm of withholding something useful — so the same data can support different conclusions, and a weak manufacturing story or an ambiguous endpoint can sink an otherwise good drug. The output is a marketing authorization with an agreed label — legal permission to sell the drug — which sends every modality on to the patient.
