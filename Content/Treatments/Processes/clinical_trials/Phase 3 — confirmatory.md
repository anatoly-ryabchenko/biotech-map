Phase 3 is the large, decisive trial that must prove, beyond reasonable doubt, that the drug's benefits outweigh its risks — the evidence a regulator will ultimately judge. It is the longest and most expensive step in all of drug development, enrolling hundreds to several thousand patients across many hospitals, often internationally, over several years.

Its power comes from its design: a [[Randomized controlled trial|randomized controlled trial]]. Patients are split _by chance_ into a group getting the new drug and a group getting either a placebo or the current standard treatment, and wherever possible neither the patients nor the assessors know who is in which group (_blinding_), so the comparison is fair and neither hope nor bias can colour the result. Success is defined _in advance_ as a specific, clinically meaningful [[Clinical endpoint|endpoint]] — living longer, a measurable clinical improvement — and the trial is made large enough, statistically _powered_, that a real effect of the expected size will stand out from chance. Fixing all this before the data arrive is what makes the result hard to argue with afterward.

For the advanced therapies, the pivotal trial is paired with years-long follow-up of treated patients, because their effects and risks unfold slowly — monitoring that continues after approval (covered at the terminal step) rather than holding up the filing.

The characteristic difficulty is that this is where most drugs reaching it still fail, and failure is ruinously expensive: a real but smaller-than-hoped benefit, a safety signal that only emerges at this scale, or a wrongly chosen endpoint or population can sink years of work and enormous cost — and because the trial is fixed in advance, there is little room to adjust once it is running. The output is the rigorous, pre-specified human evidence of benefit and risk that a regulator needs to decide on approval.
