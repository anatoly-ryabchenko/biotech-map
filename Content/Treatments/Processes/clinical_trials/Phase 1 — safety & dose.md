This is the first time the medicine enters a human being. After all the cell and animal work, Phase 1 asks the most basic question — _is it safe in people, and at what dose?_ — not yet whether it works. It is deliberately small and cautious.

For most modalities, Phase 1 enrols a small number (tens) of _healthy volunteers_, who have nothing to gain medically and are there purely to test safety. They receive the drug starting at the very low dose justified by the animal safety studies, and the dose is raised step by step across successive small groups while the team watches closely for harm — _dose escalation_. The aims are to find how much can be given before side effects become unacceptable (the _maximum tolerated dose_) and to measure the drug's [[ADME|pharmacokinetics]] in humans for the first time.

There is a crucial exception: for medicines too toxic to give a healthy person — chemotherapies, cell and gene therapies, radiopharmaceuticals — Phase 1 is run in _patients_ who have the disease, often those out of other options, so any risk is balanced by possible benefit. For these, escalation is especially careful: a classic rule treats three patients at a dose, adds more if a single serious toxicity appears, and stops escalating when too many do.

The characteristic difficulty is that this is the leap from animals to humans, where species differences first bite: a drug safe in animals can harm people in unforeseen ways, and a few first-in-human trials have caused catastrophic reactions — which is why starting doses are kept far below the animal safe level, dosing is staggered between participants, and immune-overstimulating drugs use an extra-cautious starting point. Most of what is learned here is safety and dose, not benefit. The output is a safe dose range and a human safety and pharmacokinetic profile, allowing the drug to advance to testing for effect.
