A vaccine runs through the same three rising phases as a drug, but with a defining twist: it is given to _healthy_ people to _prevent_ a disease they don't yet have, which reshapes every phase. Phase 1, in a small group of healthy adults, checks safety and whether the vaccine raises the intended immune response (measured as antibody levels). Phase 2 expands to hundreds, refining the dose and the schedule of priming and booster shots and checking the response across more varied people, still watching safety closely.

Phase 3 is where the difference is starkest. To prove a vaccine _works_, you cannot deliberately infect people; you must vaccinate a large group, give a comparison group a placebo, and then _wait for natural exposure_ — counting how many in each group catch the disease. Because only a fraction of participants will ever be exposed, these trials must be enormous, often _tens of thousands_ of people, to accumulate enough cases to show a difference. It is a [[Randomized controlled trial|randomized, controlled, blinded]] trial like any Phase 3, but sized by how rare the natural infection is. Because the recipients are healthy, the safety bar is exceptionally high — rare side effects that might be tolerated in a sick patient are unacceptable here.

The characteristic difficulty is this dependence on natural infection: a trial may drag on for years if the disease is uncommon or flares unpredictably (an outbreak can end abruptly and strand a trial), and detecting rare harms among healthy people demands huge numbers and long observation. A pre-established [[Clinical endpoint|correlate of protection]] — an antibody level known to predict protection — can sometimes stand in for waiting on cases. The output is large-scale evidence that the vaccine safely prevents disease, ready for regulatory review.
