With a safe dose range in hand, Phase 2 is the first real test of whether the drug _helps_. It enrols a larger group — typically one to a few hundred _patients_ who actually have the disease — and looks for a signal of benefit while continuing to watch safety in the population that will ultimately use the drug.

Two things are settled here. _Proof of concept_: does the drug do something useful — shrink a tumour, lower a disease marker, ease symptoms — enough to justify the enormous cost of the final trial? And _dose-finding_: several doses are often compared head-to-head to pick the one with the best balance of benefit and side effects to carry forward. In cancer this comparison is now expected rather than optional: the FDA's _Project Optimus_ asks developers to compare doses at random before the confirmatory trial, because the old habit of carrying forward the highest dose patients could tolerate often left targeted drugs at doses with more side effects but no more benefit. The measures used are early or intermediate _[[Clinical endpoint|endpoints]]_, sometimes a biomarker that stands in for the ultimate clinical benefit, chosen because a full clinical outcome would take too long or too many patients to see at this stage.

Phase 2 is sized to _estimate_ the effect, not to prove it definitively; its job is to decide go-or-no-go and to design the trial that follows. This is where the most candidates die — many drugs that looked promising in animals and proved safe in Phase 1 simply fail to show enough benefit here.

The characteristic difficulty is reading an uncertain signal from a modest number of patients without fooling yourself: an early endpoint may not predict real clinical benefit, patient-to-patient variability can mask or mimic an effect, and the temptation to over-read a hopeful trend is strong — yet getting the dose or endpoint wrong here dooms the far costlier Phase 3. The output is evidence the drug works, a chosen dose, and a defined endpoint and patient population for the confirmatory trial.
